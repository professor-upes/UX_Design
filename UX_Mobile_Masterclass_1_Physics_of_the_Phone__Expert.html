00 Cold open: the same company, two different products

Open Zomato on a laptop. Open Zomato on a phone. You are looking at one brand, one database, one set of restaurants — and two structurally different products. Not two skins. Two products.

Before you read my explanation, look at Figure 1 and write down every structural difference you can find. Not colour, not typography — structure. Where things live, what disappeared, what got added, what changed shape. Give it two minutes. The list you produce is a fairly accurate map of what you currently believe mobile design is, and the gap between your list and mine is what this masterclass closes.

What most web designers write down

Smaller screen. One column instead of three. Filters collapsed into a button. Menu moved to the bottom. Bigger tap areas. Fewer things on screen.

All correct, all surface. Every one of those observations describes an effect. None of them names a cause. That is the difference between someone who can copy a mobile layout and someone who can design one for a product that has never been designed before.

What is actually going on

Three causes generate every difference in that figure, and every other difference you will meet for the rest of your career in this domain:

  1. The hand. A thumb has an arc. That arc is roughly 60% of the screen on a modern phone, and the good part of it is at the bottom. This single fact relocated the navigation.
  2. The eye. Phone sessions are short, interrupted, one-handed, often outdoors, often in motion. That is why "28 min" appears on the phone card and not the desktop card — the phone user is deciding right now, the desktop user is browsing.
  3. The operating system. You do not own the screen. iOS and Android reserve bands at the top and bottom, own the back gesture, own the text size, and can suspend your app mid-task. On the web you owned the viewport from edge to edge. Here you are a tenant.
The thesis of this masterclass

Mobile UX is not a style. It is a set of consequences that fall out of hand, eye, and OS. If you learn the styles you will be able to imitate 2026's apps. If you learn the three constraints you will be able to derive 2031's.

The rest of this document does that derivation. Section 1 establishes the three constraints properly. Sections 2 through 6 derive the working knowledge — units, touch, navigation, layout, platform conventions — from them. Section 7 adds the constraint that most Western material ignores and that anyone shipping in India cannot: the device floor. Section 8 is the table you will actually screenshot.

Do the 2-minute exercise. Genuinely. The gap between your list and mine is the whole syllabus.

01 The three constraints: hand, eye, operating system

Everything else in this masterclass is downstream of this section. If you only read one part, read this one — and then derive the rest yourself.

1.1 — The hand

You have spent your career designing for an input device that was physically separate from the display. A mouse cursor is a one-pixel point, it never occludes the target, it can rest without clicking, and it reports a hover state that you used to build entire interaction vocabularies — tooltips, dropdown menus, preview cards, cursor changes.

A thumb is none of those things. It is a soft, wide, imprecise object attached to the same limb holding the device, and it moves in an arc, not on a plane.

Three consequences, and they are not suggestions:

Consequence 1 — Bottom navigation won on merit, not fashion

Primary navigation sits at the bottom of the screen on essentially every consumer app you can name because that is where the thumb already is. Netflix, Instagram, Spotify, Swiggy, PhonePe, CRED — bottom tabs. When Apple shipped iOS 15 they moved Safari's address bar to the bottom, which is about as strong a signal as the industry produces: the company that invented the top-of-screen browser chrome moved it down because phones got taller than hands.

You already know this as…

…the "fold." On the web you fought to get important things above the fold because scrolling had a cost. On a phone scrolling is nearly free and reaching is expensive. The axis of difficulty rotated 90 degrees. Important things do not go at the top; they go where the thumb is.

Consequence 2 — Reachability is an OS admission of design failure

iOS has "Reachability" (swipe down on the home indicator, the whole screen slides down). Android has one-handed mode. Both exist because phone height outgrew thumb length. Both are escape hatches. If your primary action requires the user to invoke an escape hatch, you have made a design error, not a device error.

Consequence 3 — The top of the screen is for information, the bottom is for action

This is the single most portable rule in this document. Titles, status, context, content — top. Buttons, tabs, confirmations, destructive actions — bottom. Look at any well-built payment sheet in a UPI app: the amount and payee are at the top where you read them, and the slide-to-pay control is at the bottom where your thumb rests.

Every primary action sits in the bottom third of the screen, or has a bottom-anchored equivalent. No exceptions for "it looked better at the top in Figma."
Teaching note — the demo that lands

Do this live. Ask students to take out their phones, hold them naturally in one hand, and — without adjusting their grip — try to tap the top-left corner. Most will physically shuffle the phone in their palm. That shuffle is the lesson; it takes eight seconds and no slide can replicate it.

Follow with: "now open any app you use daily and tell me where its main navigation is." You will get bottom tabs from nearly everyone. Let them discover the pattern rather than presenting it.

1.2 — The eye

Web design assumes a stable reader: seated, indoors, screen at a fixed 50–70cm, ambient light controlled, attention available for minutes. Phone design assumes almost none of that.

  • Viewing distance is shorter but less stable — roughly 25–35cm, and it moves. Text that is legible at rest becomes unreadable while walking.
  • Sessions are short and interrupted. Design for a user who will be pulled away mid-task and return three minutes later, or three days later. Section 4 covers what that means for state.
  • Ambient light is hostile. A phone in Delhi sun in May is competing with roughly 100,000 lux. Your carefully chosen #7A8B99 on #FFFFFF — a perfectly respectable 3.7:1 that you shipped on a website — is simply invisible outdoors.
  • Motion is normal. Reading on a moving bus, in an auto, on the metro. Fine detail and thin strokes degrade first.
The contrast number, stated precisely

WCAG 2.2 requires 4.5:1 for normal body text and 3:1 for large text (18pt, or 14pt bold) and for non-text UI components such as icons and input borders. — W3C, normative

Those are accessibility floors measured in a lab. My working rule for phone interfaces is 7:1 for anything the user must read outdoors — delivery status, OTP codes, transaction amounts, boarding passes. — my judgement, not a standard

This is also why "elegant" light-grey secondary text — the workhorse of tasteful web design — is the most common quality failure in mobile UI made by web designers. On your calibrated monitor at 30% brightness it reads as refined. On a 400-nit budget LCD at 60% brightness in daylight it reads as nothing at all.

Test every screen at maximum and minimum brightness, and once outdoors, before you call it done.

1.3 — The operating system

Here is the constraint that has no web analogue at all, and the one web designers systematically underestimate.

On the web you owned the viewport. Every pixel from 0,0 to 100vw,100vh was yours to command. The browser had chrome, but it lived outside your box.

In an app, the operating system is inside your box. It reserves regions. It owns gestures. It changes your typography without asking. It can freeze your process. And it will hand your screen to another app and give it back later in a state you have to reconstruct.

Two of these deserve immediate emphasis because they break web-trained instincts hardest.

Dynamic Type will destroy your fixed-height rows

On the web, a user zooming to 200% triggered your responsive breakpoints and the layout reflowed — you had already designed for it. On a phone, the user increases text size in system settings and your layout does not get a breakpoint. The text simply becomes larger inside the container you gave it. If you set a 44pt-tall list row with 17pt text, and the user is running 235% text size, that text is now 40pt tall inside a 44pt row. It clips, or it overlaps, or it truncates something important.

The fix is structural, not cosmetic: size containers from their contents, never the reverse. Every height in a mobile layout should be a minimum, not a value.

Your app gets suspended, not unloaded

A web page that goes away and comes back reloads. It re-runs, re-fetches, and lands on a URL that describes its state. An app that goes away and comes back resumes — sometimes from memory, sometimes from a cold launch after the OS reclaimed it. The user's mental model does not distinguish these. They expect to find their half-typed review, their scroll position, their selected filters, exactly where they left them, whether that was 30 seconds or three days ago.

The bug you will personally ship

User fills a 9-field address form. A call comes in. They return four minutes later. Fields are empty. On the web the browser would often have restored the form for you. In an app, nothing restores anything unless you wrote the code to restore it. This is the single most common quality gap between a competent app and an amateur one, and it is invisible in every design review because reviews never background the app.

Every screen with user input survives a background/foreground cycle and a cold relaunch with its state intact.
Checkpoint — derive it yourself

Question: Bottom sheets — the panels that slide up from the bottom edge — are everywhere on mobile and essentially absent on desktop web. Which of the three constraints explains this, and how?

Answer: Primarily the hand, secondarily the eye. A bottom sheet arrives inside the thumb's natural arc, so its controls are immediately reachable without a grip change. It also opens away from the hand, so the hand does not occlude its own controls — a dropdown that opened downward from a top-anchored button would be covered by the finger that opened it. On desktop neither pressure exists: a cursor reaches the top-right corner as easily as the bottom-left and never covers anything. The pattern is not a mobile aesthetic; it is a solution to a biomechanical problem desktop does not have.

Hand → where things go.
Eye → how loud they have to be.
OS → what you're allowed to assume.

The lifecycle one is the killer. Nobody reviews for it, everybody ships the bug.

If you can trace a rule back to one of these three, you understand it. If you can't, you memorised it.

02 Coordinates: pt, dp, sp — and why your CSS pixels lied

This section is short, unglamorous, and the single highest-value hour you will spend. Almost every "the design doesn't match the build" argument in mobile teams traces back to somebody not understanding what is in this section.

2.1 — The unit you thought you knew

You have been writing px for years. Here is the uncomfortable fact: the CSS pixel was never a pixel. It is a logical unit — an "anchor unit" defined so that content stays roughly the same physical size across devices, with the browser multiplying by a device pixel ratio to reach real hardware pixels. You have been using a density-independent unit this entire time and calling it by the wrong name.

Mobile platforms name it honestly:

UnitPlatformWhat it isYour web equivalent
ptiOSLogical point. Multiplied by the screen's scale factor (@1x, @2x, @3x) to reach physical pixels.CSS px, near enough
dpAndroidDensity-independent pixel. 1dp = 1px at 160dpi baseline; scaled by the device's density bucket.CSS px, near enough
spAndroidScale-independent pixel. Like dp, but also multiplied by the user's font-size setting. Use for text. Only for text.No web equivalent — rem is the closest and it isn't close
pxBothAn actual physical pixel on the panel. You almost never specify these. Asset exporters do.The thing you thought px meant

2.2 — Reference frames: pick one, name it, design in it

There is no "the phone size." There is a range, and you choose a reference device to design in and then verify at the extremes. State your reference explicitly in the file — designs that don't say what they were drawn at cause arguments.

ReferenceLogical sizeScaleUse it as
iPhone 15 / 16 (non-Pro)393 × 852 pt@3xPrimary iOS design canvas

You already know this as…

…breakpoints. But note the range: web breakpoints spanned 320px to 2560px, an 8× spread, and you built fluid systems to survive it. Phone widths span roughly 320 to 440 — a 1.4× spread. You have far less variance to handle and far less excuse for it breaking. The variance that will actually break you is vertical (text scaling, keyboard, safe areas), not horizontal.

2.3 — The 8-point grid

Spacing on both platforms runs on multiples of 8, with 4 available as a half-step for tight relationships. This is not an aesthetic preference; it is arithmetic. Multiples of 8 divide cleanly at @1x, @2x and @3x, so a 12dp gap stays crisp at every density while a 13dp gap lands on a fractional pixel and blurs its edge.

Where the discipline comes from

You already accept this in typography: you don't set a type scale of 15.3px / 17.9px / 21.4px, you set 16 / 20 / 24 and stick to it because the ratios do the work. The 8-point grid is that same argument applied to space. It is not about the number 8 — a 4-point system works too. It is about having a system so that "how much space goes here" stops being a per-instance decision.

Every margin, padding and gap in the design resolves to 4 / 8 / 16 / 24 / 32 / 48. Screen edge margin is 16dp unless you have a stated reason.
Teaching note — the grading shortcut

When marking student mockups, the fastest quality signal available is: pull the spacing values off five random elements. If they read 14, 22, 9, 17, 30, the student is eyeballing and every downstream problem is a symptom of that. If they read 8, 16, 16, 24, 8, the student has a system and you can move on to critiquing decisions rather than craft.

CSS px was always logical. You just never had to say so out loud.

sp vs dp is the one that bites. Text in sp, everything else in dp.

Seven spacing values. Print them and tape them to the monitor.

03 Touch: what happens when you delete the cursor

Moving from mouse to touch does not simplify your input model. It deletes three channels — hover, right-click, and precise pointing — and replaces them with one channel that is imprecise, occluding, and gesture-loaded. This is a harder input problem, not an easier one.

ChannelMouseTouch
Position without commitmentHover — the entire basis of tooltips, dropdown menus, preview cards, cursor affordancesGone. A finger is either touching or not. There is no "here but not yet."
Secondary actionRight-click context menuLong-press — same idea, but invisible, undiscoverable, and it competes with drag
Pointing precision~1px, exact, never occludes10–14mm contact patch, centroid drifts, hand covers the target
Multi-selectShift-click, drag-marquee, Ctrl-clickBespoke selection mode you have to design and teach
Keyboard focusTab order, free, standardExists for accessibility, invisible to most users, still your responsibility
The trap

The instinct is to "port" hover behaviours to long-press. Don't. Hover is free and reversible — the user learns it by accident, at zero cost, because their cursor was passing by anyway. Long-press costs 500ms of committed contact and is discovered by nobody who wasn't told. Anything genuinely important must have a visible, tappable path. Long-press is a shortcut for power users, never a primary route.

3.1 — The 44-point floor

The numbers. iOS: 44 × 44 pt minimum tap target. Android / Material 3: 48 × 48 dp minimum, with at least 8 dp between adjacent targets. — Apple HIG and Material 3, both normative WCAG 2.2 adds an absolute accessibility floor of 24 × 24 CSS px (SC 2.5.8, Level AA) and an enhanced target of 44 × 44 (SC 2.5.5, Level AAA). — W3C The WCAG minimum is a legal floor, not a design target; design to the platform numbers.

Why those numbers. A fingertip's contact patch on glass is roughly 10–14mm across; a thumb pad is wider still. The digitiser reports one centroid from that whole patch, and the centroid systematically drifts from where the user believes they are pointing — downward, and along the thumb's approach angle. 44pt is about 7mm of visible target with enough surrounding tolerance to absorb that drift. Below it, error rate does not decline gently; it climbs, and the errors cluster at the screen edges furthest from the thumb.

The thing web designers don't know

The touch target is not the visible element. A 24pt icon can carry a 48pt invisible hit area centred on it. This is standard, invisible to users, and it is why Apple's own toolbars look far lighter than a literal reading of the 44pt rule would allow. You do not have to make things look chunky. You have to make them hit big.

In the wild. Netflix's player controls look small — the play glyph is well under 44pt — but the hit region extends generously past the glyph in every direction. Contrast that with older builds of IRCTC Rail Connect, where the passenger-details form used checkbox targets close to the visual minimum in tightly packed rows: exactly the arrangement in the left panel of Figure 6, and exactly the mis-tap complaints you would predict.

The failure you will personally ship

A row of icon-only actions — share, bookmark, download, more — spaced by visual balance in Figma. They look correct at 100% zoom on a 27-inch monitor. In the hand, the contact patch straddles two of them and the OS picks whichever centroid is closer. Your user meant "bookmark" and got "delete." This is the most common ship-blocking bug in first mobile projects by web designers, and it is entirely invisible in the design file.

Every interactive element has a ≥44pt / 48dp hit area and ≥8dp clearance from its neighbour, regardless of its visible size.

3.2 — Occlusion: your hand covers what it touches

This one fact — obvious once said, invisible until said — generates a whole family of mobile patterns that have no web equivalent.

3.3 — The 100-millisecond contract

On the web you had :hover doing continuous work: the moment the cursor crossed a link, something changed, and the user knew the element was live before committing. That reassurance is gone. The replacement is the touch-down response, and it has a deadline.

BudgetWhat must happenWhy that number
≤ 100 msVisual response to touch-down — highlight, scale, ripple, colour shiftNielsen's threshold for "the system is reacting instantaneously." Below it the response feels caused by your finger; above it, it feels like a separate event. — NN/g
≤ 400 msThe result of the action begins to appearDoherty threshold — the point at which attention stays engaged rather than wandering. — IBM, 1982; still holds
≤ 1 sThe operation completes, or a determinate progress indicator appearsNielsen's limit for uninterrupted flow of thought
16.7 msOne frame at 60 Hz. 8.3 ms at 120 Hz.Your entire per-frame budget for layout, paint and composite. Miss it and the scroll visibly stutters.

The practical rule: every touchable element must have a pressed state, and it must fire on touch-down, not on touch-up. Material calls this the ripple; iOS uses opacity and scale. Web-trained developers often bind visual feedback to the click/tap event, which fires on release — which means the user gets 300ms of nothing, assumes the tap missed, and taps again. Double submissions in mobile checkout flows are very often this bug and not a network problem.

You already know this as…

…the 300ms click delay you fought in mobile web around 2013, where browsers waited to see whether a tap was actually a double-tap-to-zoom. You fixed it with FastClick, or later with touch-action: manipulation and a proper viewport meta tag. Native apps never had that delay — but the lesson transfers exactly: a touch interface that doesn't acknowledge contact immediately feels broken, and users respond by tapping harder and more often.

Every tappable element has a pressed state that fires on touch-down. Test it on a throttled device, not a simulator on a fast laptop.

3.4 — Gestures and the discoverability tax

Gestures are the great seduction of mobile design. Swipe to delete, pull to refresh, pinch to zoom, long-press to reorder, swipe between tabs — they feel sophisticated, they save chrome, and they are how the best apps feel fast.

They are also invisible. A gesture has no affordance. Nothing on screen says "you may swipe this row leftward." The user either already knows the convention, discovers it by accident, or never finds the feature at all.

The gesture rule

Rank a gesture by how widely it is already standardised, and match its role to that rank:

  • Universal (vertical scroll, tap, pinch-zoom on media, pull-to-refresh, edge-swipe back): safe to rely on. Users have these.
  • Common (swipe row for actions, swipe between tabs, long-press for context, drag handle to reorder): safe as an accelerator, never as the only path. Every one of these needs a visible equivalent.
  • Bespoke (your invented two-finger drag, your diagonal swipe): assume roughly nobody will find it. If you ship it, ship an onboarding hint — and accept that most users will still never use it.

CRED is the interesting Indian case here: a deliberately gesture-forward, high-craft interface that treats discovery friction as an acceptable cost because its audience is narrow, affluent, repeat-visit and self-selecting for exactly that kind of polish. That is a defensible product bet. It is a terrible default. If your app is Meesho and your next million users are first-time smartphone owners in tier-3 cities, every hidden gesture is a feature that does not exist.

Every gesture-triggered action also has a visible, tappable route to the same outcome.
Checkpoint — apply the three constraints

Question: "Swipe left on an email to delete it" is a Common gesture. What visible equivalent should exist, and where should it live?

Answer: A visible Delete control reachable without the gesture — typically inside the opened message (bottom toolbar, thumb zone) and inside a multi-select mode entered from the list. Where it must not live: a top-right overflow menu as the only route, because that is the worst point in the reach map (§1.1) for a frequent action. Note that all three constraints participate: hand decides position, eye decides that it must be a labelled icon and not a bare glyph, OS decides that it must survive a 200% text scale.

Teaching note — the 20-minute exercise that fixes this permanently

Hand out printed screenshots of a student-built mobile mockup. Give each student a red pen and a cut-out 44pt square at the printout's scale. Ask them to place the square over every interactive element and mark every one that doesn't fit, and every pair whose squares overlap. Ten minutes, and the number never goes below three on a first attempt. It converts an abstract rule into a physical count, and students stop arguing with it.

No hover. No right-click. No precision. Three channels gone, one added.

Hit area ≠ visual bounds. If you take one line from this section, take that one.

Occlusion explains bottom sheets, action sheets, bottom toasts, flipped menus — all of it.

Feedback on touch-DOWN. Not on release.

04 Navigation architecture — the structural difference

If Section 1 is the physics, this is the engineering. Navigation is where web experience helps you least and misleads you most, because on the web the platform did almost all of this work for you and you never had to notice.

4.1 — What the URL was doing for you

On the web, navigation state lives in a string. That one design decision gave you, for free:

  • A back button that always worked, implemented once by the browser, consistent across every site on the internet.
  • Shareable, bookmarkable state. Any point in your app could be sent over WhatsApp and it would open there.
  • Restorable position. Refresh and you land where you were, more or less.
  • A mental model users already had. Pages. Forward and back. Nobody needed teaching.

In an app, none of that is free. Navigation is a data structure you build and maintain. Specifically, it is a stack — you push screens onto it and pop them off — and if you want deep links, state restoration or a sane back behaviour, you write them.

The rule the transition is encoding

Horizontal push = deeper into the same journey. Back returns you. Context is preserved.
Vertical modal = a self-contained interruption. It has its own Cancel/Done, it doesn't get a Back arrow, and it must be dismissible. Login, compose, share, permissions, filters.

When a user says an app "feels confusing" and can't say why, this is the cause about a third of the time. They have lost track of whether they are inside something or on top of it.

4.2 — Tabs are parallel stacks, not links

The most common structural error web designers make: treating a bottom tab bar like a website's top nav — five links, one page at a time, tapping a tab replaces the content.

That is not what a tab bar is. Each tab owns its own independent navigation stack, and those stacks persist. If the user is three screens deep in Search, switches to Profile, and comes back to Search, they should land exactly where they were — three deep, same scroll position. Instagram, Netflix, Spotify and Swiggy all do this. It is the behaviour users have been trained on for a decade, and violating it makes an app feel cheap in a way that is hard for a non-designer to articulate.

You already know this as…

…nothing, actually. This has no web equivalent. The closest thing is multiple browser tabs, and the analogy is genuinely useful: think of each bottom tab as its own browser tab with its own history. It is one of the very few concepts in this masterclass where your web experience gives you no head start at all — which is precisely why it's worth flagging.

4.3 — The navigation container decision

Four containers, four jobs. This is the decision, not a menu of styles:

Why the hamburger lost — the honest version

The common telling is "hamburger menus are bad." The accurate telling is more useful: a hamburger menu trades discoverability for space, and on a phone's primary navigation that trade is almost always wrong. Contents hidden behind an icon get visited less than contents visible on a tab bar — this has been replicated repeatedly in navigation research, and the industry's collective migration to bottom tabs from roughly 2014 onward is the practical verdict.

But it lost as primary navigation only. Drawers remain correct where the destination list is long, hierarchical, and not the thing you switch between every ten seconds. Gmail's account and label drawer is right. Obsidian's file-tree drawer is right. Slack's workspace switcher is right. The pattern isn't dead; its job description got narrower.

4.4 — Three legitimate answers: Netflix, Notion, Obsidian

Three well-designed apps solve the same problem — "how does a user get anywhere" — in three incompatible ways, and each is correct for its content shape. This is the section to study if you want to stop copying patterns and start choosing them.

Netflix — flat, wide, browse-not-navigate

Netflix's catalogue is enormous but structurally flat: there is no meaningful hierarchy between "a thriller from 2019" and "a comedy from 2023." Users don't navigate to a title, they encounter one. So the architecture optimises for encounter: a small fixed set of bottom tabs, a home surface that is almost entirely algorithmic rows, and a detail screen that is at most two levels deep. The player then removes all chrome entirely, because during playback the content is the interface.

Note what this buys and costs. It buys speed to content and near-zero navigation learning. It costs precision — finding one specific title you half-remember is genuinely worse on Netflix than in a file browser, which is why Search is a first-class tab rather than a header icon.

Notion — deep tree compressed into a stack

Notion has the hardest mobile problem of the three. Its desktop product is a dense, infinitely nestable tree with a sidebar, multi-column layouts, hover-revealed controls, keyboard shortcuts and drag-and-drop as core interaction. Essentially every one of those relies on capabilities the phone deleted.

The mobile answer is a push/pop stack with a breadcrumb strip standing in for the sidebar's spatial sense — and, more importantly, a deliberate reduction in what mobile is for. Mobile Notion is weighted toward reading, quick capture and light editing; heavy structural work stays on desktop. That is the professional move, and it is the one students resist most: you are allowed to decide that some of your product does not belong on the phone.

The compression question

Every desktop-first product hits this. Ask it explicitly: which 20% of this product accounts for 80% of the moments a user would reach for their phone? Build that, properly. Then decide, on purpose, what the phone will simply not do. A mobile app that does four things excellently beats one that does forty things at 40%.

Obsidian — the principled exception

Obsidian's mobile app puts a file-tree drawer where the orthodoxy says a tab bar belongs. By the rules in §4.3 this should be wrong. It isn't, and understanding why is the point of including it.

Obsidian's content is a user-authored filesystem. There are no fixed top-level destinations to put in a tab bar — the destinations are whatever folders you made. A tab bar would either be empty of meaning or would be four generic labels sitting on top of the actual navigation, adding a layer rather than removing one. The drawer's discoverability cost is also much lower here than for a mass-market app, because Obsidian's users chose a local-first Markdown tool on purpose and arrive with a filesystem mental model already loaded.

Generalise it

A drawer is defensible when the destinations are created by the user rather than by you, and when your users are self-selected for depth. It is indefensible when you have a fixed set of five destinations you designed yourself and hid behind an icon to keep the screen tidy.

4.5 — Back: two platforms, two contracts

This is where cross-platform apps built by web teams break most visibly.

Back is designed per platform, not once. On Android, verify: keyboard → sheet → screen → tab-to-home → exit, in that order.

4.6 — Deep links and state restoration

The URL gave you shareable state for free. In an app you get it by building it: a deep link arrives, and you must synthesise a plausible back stack so the user who opened "Bikanervala" from a WhatsApp message can press back and land somewhere sensible rather than being dumped out of the app.

Practically, this means every screen worth linking to needs: a stable identifier, a rule for what its parent stack should be when arrived at cold, and a loading state for the case where it was opened with no cached data. Web designers get this wrong by assuming — reasonably, from a decade of habit — that "it has a URL, so it works."

Every shareable screen defines its synthetic back stack and its cold-open loading state.
Checkpoint — diagnose the architecture

Question: You are designing the mobile app for a hospital chain. Users need: book appointment, view reports, prescriptions, past visits, a family-members switcher, insurance, and support. Tab bar, drawer, or both — and how do you decide?

Answer: Both, split by frequency and ownership. The tab bar takes the small set of high-frequency destinations users switch between — Appointments, Reports, Prescriptions, Profile — because those are yours, fixed, and visited repeatedly. The family-members switcher is not a destination at all; it is a scope control that changes what every tab shows, so it belongs in the header as a persistent selector (like Slack's workspace switcher), not in the tab bar. Insurance and Support are low-frequency and belong inside Profile. Note the diagnostic sequence: count destinations, classify by frequency, then separate "destinations" from "scope controls" — a distinction that does not exist on the web, where a scope change was just another URL.

Teaching note — the assignment that teaches this best

Give students one content shape and ask for three architectures. For example: "a college's student app — attendance, timetable, fees, library, events, grievances, hostel." Have them produce a tab-bar version, a drawer version, and a hybrid, each with a written justification. Then have them argue. The productive discovery is that there is no single right answer, only a set of trade-offs that depend on which two things students actually open daily. Push them to go find out rather than guess — five interviews is enough to settle it.

The URL was doing enormous unpaid work for you. Now you pay for it.

Tabs = parallel stacks that persist. This one has no web analogue at all.

Horizontal = deeper. Vertical = interruption. Never mix the signals.

05 Layout and density — the inversion

One column is not a compromise you make because the screen is narrow. It is the format. And the economics of the format are the inverse of the web's.

5.1 — Scrolling got cheap, tapping got expensive

On the web you optimised against scrolling. Above-the-fold was a real concern, you fought for vertical space, you compressed content into columns, and a click was a trivially cheap action — one pixel-perfect, instantaneous, zero-effort event.

On a phone the economics flip completely:

On the web

Scroll = expensive. Requires a wheel, a drag, or a keypress. Users genuinely stopped at the fold.

Click = cheap. Precise, instant, no error rate, no travel cost. Deep navigation trees were viable.

Result: wide, shallow, dense. Cram it in, link liberally.

On a phone

Scroll = nearly free. A flick of the thumb travels hundreds of points. Users scroll enormous distances without noticing.

Tap = expensive. Aim, commit, wait for a screen transition, lose your place, need a way back.

Result: narrow, deep-in-content, shallow-in-navigation. Show more per screen, make them tap less.

The design consequence people get backwards

Because taps are expensive, you should generally push more information onto the current screen rather than behind another tap. The instinct to "simplify" by hiding details behind a chevron is often a web instinct misapplied — on the web that chevron cost a cheap click; here it costs an expensive one.

This is why Swiggy's restaurant card shows rating, cuisine, price-for-two, delivery time and offer all at once. Five data points on a card looks busy in a design review. It is correct, because each one it hides is a tap plus a back-navigation plus a lost scroll position.

5.2 — The Netflix rail: why horizontal carousels work here and fail on desktop

Horizontal carousels have a genuinely poor reputation in web design — low engagement past the first item, poor accessibility, users ignore the arrows. On mobile they are the dominant pattern in streaming, commerce and food delivery. Both facts are true, and the reason is worth working through properly rather than accepting as a quirk.

Every horizontal rail clips its last visible item, and is inset ≥16dp from the screen edge.

5.3 — The Notion problem: compressing a dense tool

Notion on desktop leans on multi-column layouts, a persistent sidebar, hover-revealed block handles, right-click menus, drag-and-drop block reordering and a deep keyboard-shortcut vocabulary. Count how many of those survive contact with a phone: the sidebar has nowhere to live, hover is gone (§3), right-click is gone, drag-and-drop competes with scroll, and the keyboard shortcuts require a keyboard.

5.4 — Typography that survives a phone

The web habit that breaks hardest

A 12px caption in light grey is a completely normal, professional choice on a website. On a phone it is simultaneously below the comfortable reading floor, likely failing contrast, and about to be scaled to 28pt by a user with Dynamic Type turned up — inside a row you sized at 44pt. It fails three ways at once. If you catch yourself reaching for small light grey text, that is the moment to stop and re-read §1.2.

No text below 13pt/sp. No body text below 15. Every text container sized from content, never fixed.
Checkpoint — the density question

Question: A product manager asks you to "clean up" a busy restaurant card by moving rating and delivery time behind a tap. Do you agree? On what grounds?

Answer: No, and the grounds are the tap/scroll inversion in §5.1. Rating and delivery time are precisely the two variables the user is comparing across cards — the decision is comparative, and comparison requires simultaneous visibility. Hiding them converts one scroll-and-scan into n taps and n back-navigations. "Clean" is not the objective; time-to-decision is. The correct move is to reduce visual noise around those data points (weight, colour, spacing) rather than to remove them.

Scroll cheap, tap expensive. The whole section is downstream of this one sentence.

Rails work here for a reason. Always clip the last card.

06 iOS versus Android — conventions, and when to break them

You are designing for two operating systems with different idioms, different component libraries, and different user expectations built over fifteen years. Pretending otherwise is the most common way a cross-platform app feels subtly wrong on both.

6.2 — The real question: unified or native?

This is genuinely contested. Both positions are held by competent people shipping successful products, so here is the argument on both sides rather than a verdict.

The case for unified

Your brand is the product. Users who switch phones should recognise your app. One design system, one component library, one set of specs, roughly half the design and QA cost. Users are far more fluent in cross-platform conventions than they were in 2015 — a whole generation learned interfaces from apps, not from an OS.

Who does this: Netflix, Spotify, Instagram, Uber, most large consumer apps.

The case for native

Your app is a guest in an environment the user knows better than they know you. Matching platform idioms makes it feel fast, familiar and trustworthy — and trust is not a brand asset you can substitute for. Native components also inherit accessibility, text scaling, dark mode and future OS changes for free.

Who does this: Banking and government apps, utilities, Apple's and Google's own apps, most productivity tools.

The decision framework I'd actually use

Unify the brand layer. Localise the system layer.

  • Unify: colour, typography, iconography, illustration, motion personality, content layout, tone of voice, your own components (cards, rails, product tiles).
  • Localise, always: navigation and back behaviour, share sheet, date and time pickers, permission prompts, keyboard handling, text scaling, haptics, system dialogs, destructive-action confirmation.

Look again at Figure 13 through this lens: the switch colour should be your brand's. The switch shape and position should be the platform's. That single split resolves most of the argument.

The failure mode at each extreme is worth naming. Fully unified apps built by web teams tend to ship an Android app that is an iOS app with the corners changed — no system back handling, no FAB where one belongs, iOS-style action sheets where a dialog is expected. Fully native apps built without a design system tend to drift into two products that share a logo and nothing else, and the cost surfaces two years later when every feature has to be designed twice.

Navigation, back, pickers, permissions and share are platform-native. Everything above them is yours.
Teaching note — the assessment that exposes the gap

Give students an iOS screenshot of a well-known app and ask them to redraw it as the Android version. The weak submissions change the status bar and the font. The strong ones move the back affordance, swap the grouped inset list for flat sections, replace the action sheet with a dialog, and add a FAB — and can say why for each. It marks itself, and it's a good mid-term.

Unify the brand layer. Localise the system layer. That's the whole answer.

"Android version" ≠ "iOS version with different corners."

Elevation vs materials: Android stacks above, iOS sees through.

07 The Indian device floor

Most mobile UX material is written from a context where the reference device is a recent iPhone on reliable LTE. If you are shipping in India, that describes a minority of your users. The constraint is real, it is a design constraint rather than an engineering one, and it changes decisions.

7.1 — What the floor actually looks like

DimensionDesign-review deviceRealistic floor device
Screen6.1" OLED, @3x, 1000+ nits6.5" LCD, @2x, 720 × 1600 px, ~400 nits — and it will be used outdoors
Memory8 GB2–4 GB, of which your app gets a fraction before the OS reclaims it
Storage256 GB, mostly free32–64 GB, chronically near full — users uninstall apps to free space, and yours competes
NetworkStable 5G / office Wi-Fi4G that degrades to 3G on a moving train; frequent 2–5 s round trips; metered data
Background workStandard OS lifecycleAggressive OEM battery managers that kill background processes and suppress notifications
Text size setting100%Frequently raised — shared devices, older users, small-print fatigue
LanguageEnglishEnglish plus one or more Indic scripts, which are typographically taller and wider than Latin

7.2 — What this changes in your design

Image strategy is a design decision, not an engineering one

The designer decides the aspect ratio, the crop, the number of images per screen and whether a screen can be understood before its images arrive. A rail of six 3:4 posters at @3x is a meaningfully different product from a rail of six 16:9 thumbnails, in bytes and in time-to-usable. JioHotstar and Netflix both ship aggressive image sizing and progressive loading in India for exactly this reason — the poster you see first is often a low-resolution placeholder that sharpens.

Skeletons must be shaped, not generic

A grey rectangle where the title will be, a grey square where the poster will be, in the real layout. This does two things: it removes the layout shift when content arrives, and it tells the user what is coming. A centred spinner does neither and, past about three seconds, is indistinguishable from a crash.

Offline is a state you design, not an error you show

On a train between Delhi and Dehradun, connectivity is intermittent by default, not exceptionally. The question "what does this screen look like with no network and stale cache" needs an answer for every screen, and the answer is rarely "a full-screen error." Usually it is: show the cached content, mark it as stale, queue the user's actions, and reconcile when the connection returns. Obsidian is instructive here for a different reason — it is local-first by architecture, so its offline state is simply its normal state.

Install size is a conversion metric

On a phone with 3 GB free, a 90 MB app competes with photographs. Google Play's compressed download ceiling for app bundles is 200 MB, but that is a technical limit rather than a target — this market has produced a genuine culture of "Lite" builds precisely because size affects whether the install completes at all. Design consequence: every bundled font, illustration set, animation library and onboarding video is a trade against install completion.

The reframe worth teaching

Designing for the floor is not designing down. Every one of these moves — shaped skeletons, text-first loading, cached-and-stale states, optimistic UI, restrained asset budgets — makes the app better on a flagship too. The flagship user simply never notices, because the improvement is invisible when everything is fast. Constraints on the floor produce quality at the ceiling.

Every screen has a defined empty, loading, stale-cache, offline and error state — designed, not defaulted.

2 GB RAM, 720p, metered 4G, near-full storage. That's your user.

Skeletons shaped like the content. Text before images. Cache and refresh.

Designing for the floor is how you get quality at the ceiling.

09 Studio work

Reading this document changes nothing. These two exercises are where it becomes usable. Budget three hours.

Exercise 1 — Teardown · 60 minutes · individual

Pick one Indian app you use several times a week. Swiggy, Zomato, PhonePe, Groww, Blinkit, IRCTC Rail Connect, JioHotstar, Rapido — pick one you actually use, not one you admire.

  1. Map the navigation stack. Draw it as a diagram in the style of Figure 8. Start at cold launch. Show every tab as a separate stack. Mark which transitions are horizontal pushes and which are vertical modals. Go at least three levels deep on your most-used path.
  2. Overlay the reach map on three screens: the home screen, the screen where the primary conversion happens, and one settings or account screen. Mark every interactive element as natural / stretch / hard using Figure 2's zones.
  3. Find three measurable violations. Not "this feels cluttered." Each one must name the number it breaks and where the number comes from — for example: "The 'Apply coupon' control is 32 × 24pt against a 44pt iOS minimum, and it sits in the top-right hard zone while being the second-most-used control on the screen."
  4. Propose one fix for the worst of the three, and state which constraint your fix serves and which trade-off you accepted.
Marking criterion

A submission that says "the UI is confusing" scores nothing. A submission that says "the back gesture is consumed by the offers carousel on the restaurant screen, so a one-handed user cannot leave without reaching the top-left corner" scores full marks even if it identifies only one problem. Specificity is the skill being assessed.

Exercise 2 — Rebuild · 90 minutes · individual, then paired critique

Take one screen from a website you have built yourself — a portfolio project counts, a client site counts, a college project counts. Redesign it as a phone screen at 393 × 852 pt.

Deliver three things:

  • The screen, drawn to the 8-point grid, with safe areas marked and every hit area annotated.
  • A change log — one line per structural change, each naming the constraint that forced it. Aim for at least eight entries. If you have fewer than eight, you have shrunk the page rather than redesigned it.
  • A "what I removed and why" paragraph. Removing things on purpose is the skill; removing nothing is the failure mode.

Then swap with a partner and run their screen against the checklist below. Do not discuss it first — mark it cold, the way a user meets it.

The master checklist

Every checklist line from this masterclass, in one place. This is what a screen has to survive before it leaves your hands.

Hand — position and reach

☐ Primary action in the bottom third, or bottom-anchored
☐ Nothing critical in the top-far corner
☐ Menus and sheets open away from the finger
☐ Horizontal rails inset ≥16dp from the screen edge
☐ Destructive actions deliberately placed out of the easy arc

Touch — targets and feedback

☐ Every interactive element ≥44pt / 48dp hit area
☐ ≥8dp clearance between adjacent targets
☐ Pressed state fires on touch-down, within 100 ms

☐ No behaviour depends on hover or right-click

☐ State survives background, resume and cold relaunch

☐ Modals use Cancel/Done, never a back arrow

Teaching note — running this in a 90-minute class

Exercise 1 works well as pre-class homework — students arrive with their teardown and the session becomes a critique studio rather than a lecture. Twelve students × three violations gives you thirty-six real examples from Indian apps they use, which is a far better slide deck than anything you would build in advance.

For Exercise 2, insist on paper or a whiteboard for the first thirty minutes. Students who open Figma immediately spend the hour on corner radii. The structural decision — what goes where, what gets cut — is made faster and better with a pen, and that is the decision being taught.

If you have only one session, cut Exercise 2 to the change log alone. The eight-entry list is where the learning is concentrated; the pixels are optional.

10 Vocabulary, sources, and what comes next

10.1 — Glossary

TermMeaning
Safe areaThe region of the screen not covered by system UI — status bar, notch or Dynamic Island, home indicator, rounded corners. Read from the system; never hardcode.
InsetThe distance between the screen edge and the safe area on a given side. Varies by device and orientation.
Hit area / hit boxThe invisible region that responds to touch. May be, and usually should be, larger than the visible element.
Navigation stackThe ordered list of screens the user has pushed into. Back pops the top item. Each tab owns its own.
Push / popAdding a screen to the stack (usually a horizontal slide) and removing it. Signals depth within one journey.
Modal presentationA screen presented over the stack, usually vertically, that must be resolved or dismissed. Gets Cancel/Done, not Back.
Bottom sheetA panel rising from the bottom edge carrying contextual choices. Occupies the thumb zone and avoids occlusion.
RailA horizontally scrolling row of items inside a vertically scrolling screen. The Netflix pattern.
ptLogical point (iOS). Multiplied by the screen's scale factor to reach physical pixels.
dpDensity-independent pixel (Android). 1dp = 1px at 160dpi.
spScale-independent pixel (Android). Like dp, but multiplied by the user's font-size setting. Text only.
DPR / scale factorHow many physical pixels draw one logical unit — @1x, @2x, @3x.
Dynamic TypeiOS's user-controlled text sizing. The iOS counterpart to Android's font scale.
ReachabilityiOS gesture that slides the whole screen down so the top becomes thumb-reachable. An escape hatch, not a design allowance.
OcclusionThe hand covering the part of the screen it is touching. The cause of most bottom-anchored mobile patterns.
Optimistic UIShowing the result of a user action immediately and reconciling with the server afterwards. Essential on slow networks.
Skeleton screenA placeholder in the shape of the incoming content, shown during loading. Replaces the spinner.
State restorationRebuilding the exact screen, scroll position and input state after the OS suspends or kills the app.
Deep linkA URL that opens a specific screen inside the app, requiring a synthesised back stack.
Elevation vs materialsAndroid's depth model (tonal shift plus shadow, layers above) versus iOS's (blur and vibrancy, layers seen through).

10.2 — Sources, and how to read them

Where this masterclass states a platform number — 44pt, 48dp, 8dp spacing, 34pt home indicator — it comes from the platform guidelines and is normative. Where it states a research finding, the source and its date are given so you can judge how much weight it carries. Where it states my own working rule, it is labelled as judgement. Please keep that distinction when you teach from this, because students will quote whatever you say with equal confidence.

  • Apple Human Interface Guidelines — the authority for iOS. Read the Layout, Navigation, and Accessibility sections at minimum. Updated with each OS release; check it rather than trusting a blog post about it.
  • Material Design 3 — the authority for Android. The Accessibility and Navigation sections carry the touch-target and structure guidance used here.
  • WCAG 2.2 (W3C, 2023) — SC 1.4.3 and 1.4.11 for contrast, SC 2.5.5 and 2.5.8 for target size.
  • Nielsen Norman Group — the response-time thresholds (0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s) and a large body of mobile navigation and discoverability research.
  • Steven Hoober, How Do Users Really Hold Mobile Devices? (2013) — the grip ratios in Figure 2. Directionally useful, geometrically dated; the caveat in the figure is not optional.
  • Luke Wroblewski, Mobile First (2011) — thumb-zone thinking and the argument for constraint-led design. Old, and still the clearest statement of the case.
  • Josh Clark, Designing for Touch (2015) — the best single treatment of touch ergonomics for designers.
  • Refactoring UI, Wathan & Schoger (2018) — spacing systems, hierarchy through weight and colour, and why constraint-based scales work.

10.3 — What Masterclass 2 covers, and why it comes second

This masterclass built the structure: where things go, how big they are, how the user moves between them, and what the platform demands. Everything in it is static — it would be true of a printed screenshot.

Masterclass 2 is Motion, State and Feel: transitions and what they communicate, easing and spring physics, the five states every screen has (empty, loading, partial, error, ideal), perceived performance, optimistic UI, haptics, and gesture-driven interaction where the animation tracks your finger rather than playing back at you.

It comes second for a simple reason. Motion communicates relationships between structures. A shared-element transition tells the user that the small poster they tapped and the large poster now filling the screen are the same object — but only if there is a coherent structure for it to describe. Animating an incoherent architecture produces an app that feels slick for ten seconds and confusing thereafter. Structure first, then feel.

Masterclass 3 is Systems and Surfaces: design tokens and component libraries, onboarding and permission priming, recommendation and feed surfaces, power-user density, monetisation placement, and the handoff from design to engineering.

One last thing

You came into this with a decade of design instinct built on a cursor and a wide canvas. Almost none of that instinct is wasted — hierarchy, typography, rhythm, colour, restraint all transfer intact. What does not transfer is the geometry: where the input comes from, what it covers, how big it is, and who owns the screen.

That is a small set of things to relearn, and Figure 15 fits on one page. Go rebuild one of your own screens. That is where it lands.

Structure first. Then motion. Then systems. In that order, for a reason.

The instinct transfers. The geometry doesn't. That's the whole gap.
