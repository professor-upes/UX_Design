Field notes · website 103 · sheet b

Ten questions that find what's broken

Sheet 00 gave you the skeleton. Sheet A gave you spacing and hierarchy. Both taught you to build. This one teaches you to judge — including your own page, which is much harder, because you already know where everything is. Ten questions, written in 1994, that still work on interfaces nobody had imagined then.

Nielsen's 10 · use them on your own page first

Where the ten came from, and why the pedigree matters

Jakob Nielsen and Rolf Molich published the method in 1990. Four years later Nielsen cut the list to ten by factor-analysing 249 documented usability problems and keeping the principles with the most explanatory power over that corpus. That is not a list of things an expert felt strongly about. It is a compression: given 249 real ways interfaces had failed real people, these ten categories accounted for the most variance.

This is why they've outlived the interfaces they were written for. They describe mismatches between humans and machines, not features of any screen. "Visibility of system status" applies identically to a 1994 dialog box, a Blinkit rider moving on a map, and an AI assistant streaming a half-finished sentence at you.

The ten names below are Nielsen's. Everything else in this notebook — the screens, the failures, the arguments, the Indian examples — is mine, rebuilt for teaching. The demo brand KOSA is the same fictional D2C store from sheet 00, so we can point at exact pixels without pretending we audited someone's live checkout this morning.

Part one — the sheet itself
01 / the ten

A cheat sheet is only useful if it's phrased as a question

Nielsen's names are labels for categories. Labels don't find bugs. Questions find bugs. So here is the sheet twice — his name on the left, the sentence you actually say out loud while staring at a screen in the middle.

#Nielsen's nameThe question you actually askWhat the failure looks like
1Visibility of system statusRight now, does the screen tell me what it's doing — and roughly how long it'll take?A spinner with no text. A payment page that goes blank. No confirmation after you click.

Why they're vague, and why that's the point

Students always ask for the specific version — "how many pixels", "which exact words". There isn't one, deliberately. A heuristic is a rule of thumb: broad enough to apply to a screen its author never saw, narrow enough to still be falsifiable on a given page. The moment you write "the cart icon must be 24px in the top right", the rule dies with the next interface generation.

So the ten are not a spec. They're a set of angles to look from. The skill you're building is not memorising them — it's being able to look at your own checkout and say "this fails 5 and 9, severity 3" in a sentence your client can act on.

02 / the method

Two passes, three evaluators, twenty minutes each

A heuristic evaluation is a procedure, not a vibe. Run it wrong and you get ten opinions. Run it right and you get a ranked, actionable defect list before you've spent a rupee on users.

The procedure

  • Pick one task, not "the site". "Buy a 30ml serum, guest checkout, UPI." Heuristics evaluate a path. A site is not a path.
  • Pass one — flow. Walk the task end to end, once, without stopping to write. You are learning the shape of the thing. Resist annotating.
  • Pass two — elements. Walk it again slowly, screen by screen, running all ten questions against each screen. Write every finding, even ones you're unsure about.
  • Evaluate alone. Merge later. This is the rule people break. If three of you discuss while evaluating, you converge, and you'll all miss the same things. Independence is where the coverage comes from.
  • Write findings in one sentence each: what you saw + which heuristic + severity + one suggested fix. Anything longer is a report nobody reads.
  • Severity-rate after merging, not while finding. Rating as you go makes you argue with yourself instead of looking.

Nielsen's severity scale — use it exactly as written

RatingMeansDo what about it
0Not a usability problemNothing. Keeping 0s in your sheet is honest — it shows you considered and rejected it.
1Cosmetic onlyFix if there's spare time before ship. Never blocks.
2Minor problemLow priority. Batch these into a cleanup sprint.
3Major problemHigh priority. Fix before launch.
4Usability catastropheImperative. Ship date moves if this doesn't get fixed.

Severity is not "how ugly". It's a product of three things: how often it happens, how badly it hurts when it does, and how persistent it is — can the user learn their way past it, or do they hit it every single time? A tiny annoyance on the checkout button that 100% of buyers hit every order outranks a broken layout on a page nobody visits.

The honest version of that curve — grade the evidence, don't just quote it

That 35%/75% shape comes from a mathematical model Nielsen and Landauer fitted in 1993, averaged over their own case studies. It is a planning estimate, not a law of nature, and three findings should keep you humble about it:

FindingWhat it actually showed
Faulkner, 2003
(user testing, not HE)		60 users tested; random sets of 5 drawn 100 times. The average set found 85% of known problems — but individual sets ranged from 99% down to 55%. Ten users lifted the worst case to ~80%; twenty users to ~95%. The average was fine. The variance was the story.
Spool & Schroeder, 2001;
Perfetti & Landesman		On large, complex e-commerce sites, five users were nowhere near 85%. In one study roughly a third of the issues had surfaced after five users, and serious ones were still appearing at eighteen.
The evaluator effect
(Hertzum & Jacobsen)		Across a set of published studies, any two evaluators inspecting the same interface with the same heuristics agreed on as little as 5% and at best about 65% of findings. Same screen, same ten rules, largely different lists.

What to teach from this: heuristic evaluation is a cheap, fast sweep that finds a lot of real problems and misses a lot of real problems. It tells you what looks broken. It cannot tell you what users actually do, and it cannot tell you what any of it costs you in rupees. It is the thing you run before you spend money on testing — never the thing you run instead.

Part two — status and language
03 / heuristics 1 & 2

A spinner is a promise. Most spinners are lying.

These two travel together because they fail together. Heuristic 1 says the screen must keep telling you what's happening. Heuristic 2 says it must do that in your language, not the server's. A page can satisfy the first and still fail the second — that's what an error code is: perfect visibility of a status nobody can read.

The three time limits behind heuristic 1

Nielsen's response-time thresholds are the mechanism underneath "keep the user informed", and they haven't moved since the 1960s because they're properties of human attention, not of hardware:

DelayWhat it feels likeWhat the interface owes you
0.1 sInstant — the click caused itNothing. Just don't be slower.
1 sNoticeable, but thought is unbrokenNo indicator needed, but the transition shouldn't be jarring.
10 sThe edge of attention — people leave or switch tabsA determinate indicator: percentage, step count, or "3 of 5". A spinner is not enough past this point.

Practical rule for the kind of connections your users actually have: anything you expect to take more than a second gets a state on screen; anything past ten seconds gets a progress indicator that moves and can be read. If you can't measure progress, say what's happening in words and change the words as it goes. "Checking stock" → "Reserving your item" → "Confirming with your bank" is three honest sentences and it beats every spinner on earth.

Part three — exits and expectations

5
One action, one word, every screen: Remove. The broken flow calls the same thing Trash, then Delete, then OK — three vocabularies for one idea, which forces the user to re-learn on every screen.a product's words are its signposting. pick one and never swap it.

The two kinds of consistency — and which one wins

KindWhat it meansWhere students get it wrong
InternalYour own site agrees with itself: same button style, same word for the same action, same date format, same spacing scale.Building each page separately. By page four, the primary button has three different paddings and two different greens.
ExternalYour site agrees with the rest of the internet: cart at top right, logo goes home, ✕ closes, hamburger opens navigation, back goes back.Treating a convention as a chance to be original. Moving the cart to the left is not a design decision, it's a tax on every visitor.

Jakob's Law, and why originality is expensive here

Nielsen's own one-liner: users spend most of their time on other sites. Every expectation your visitor arrives with was built somewhere else — on Amazon, on WhatsApp, on a UPI app. Your interface is being read through habits you didn't create and can't reset.

So conventions are not a lack of imagination. They're free comprehension, and you get to spend your originality on the parts that actually differentiate you — the photography, the copy, the product itself. The layout of the cart icon is not where a D2C brand wins.

The India-specific one your students will miss: number formatting. ₹1,19,900 is the Indian grouping; ₹119,900 is the Western one. Getting this wrong on a price makes an otherwise polished page feel subtly foreign — and it's a one-line fix with toLocaleString('en-IN'). Same for dates: 15/08/2026 reads as 15 August here and as nothing at all in the US. If a number can be misread, spell the month.

When you must confirm instead of offering undo

Undo is the default, but it needs the action to be reversible. Money that has left an account, an SMS that has been sent, a booking that released a seat back to the pool — none of these can be pulled back, so they get a real confirmation. When you write one, three rules:

  • Name the consequence, not the action. "Cancel this order? The ₹1,199 refund reaches your bank in 5–7 days" beats "Are you sure?"
  • Label the buttons with verbs. "Cancel order" and "Keep order" — never OK/Cancel, which in a cancellation dialog is genuinely ambiguous in both directions.
  • Make the safe choice the easy one — but don't hide the destructive one. Hiding it is a dark pattern; de-emphasising it is good design. The difference is whether a determined user can still find it in one look.
Part four — prevention and memory
05 / heuristics 5 & 6

The best error message is the one that was impossible to trigger

5

Two different failures wearing the same red border

Don Norman's distinction is the one that makes heuristic 5 usable, because the two kinds of error need opposite fixes:

TypeWhat happenedWhat fixes it
SlipRight intention, wrong execution. They meant 248001, typed 24801. They meant Remove on item two, tapped item three.Physical and structural fixes: bigger targets, spacing between destructive and safe actions, input masks, pickers, undo.
MistakeWrong intention. They believed COD was available, believed the coupon stacked, believed "Save" also submitted the order.Language and feedback fixes: clearer labels, showing consequences before the action, better help. No amount of validation catches a mistake.

Diagnosing which one you're looking at decides where the fix goes. A team that treats every error as a slip just keeps adding red borders to a page whose model is what confused people.

The memory number everyone quotes, and what it actually supports

Someone in your class will say "seven plus or minus two". Miller's 1956 paper is real, but it's about immediate recall of unrelated items in a lab, and it has been stretched to justify everything from menu length to nav items — none of which it says. Later work puts the working-memory span closer to four chunks, and it varies with the person, the material and whether they're distracted.

So don't teach a number. Teach the operational version, which is much stronger and needs no citation: any piece of information a user has to carry from screen A to screen B in their head is a design defect — a coupon code, an order ID, a price they were quoted, an OTP, the name of the plan they chose. Carry it for them, on screen, at the moment it's needed. On a phone, where switching to the SMS app to read an OTP destroys the page state, this is not a nicety.

What "aesthetic and minimalist" actually claims

Nielsen's original wording is an argument about relative visibility, not about taste: content that is irrelevant or rarely needed shouldn't share the screen with content that matters, because attention is finite and every extra element takes a share of it. Rephrased for a design review, the heuristic is:

  • It is not "use less colour" or "add more whitespace". A dense Bloomberg terminal can pass this heuristic; a page with three fonts and one sentence can fail it.
  • It is a question about competition at a moment. Pick the user's task at that screen. Now list everything visible that isn't serving it. That list is your finding.
  • The fix is usually sequencing, not deletion. Most "clutter" is information someone genuinely needs — just not now. Move it behind an accordion, a second step, or a "see all offers" link. This is progressive disclosure, and it is how you satisfy 7 and 8 at once.

The best argument against this chapter — make the class defend it

Open Flipkart, Nykaa or Myntra next to a US or European store in the same category. Ours are denser, by a lot: bank offers, EMI options, exchange value, COD availability, delivery date by pincode, coupon stacks, and the statutory block we found in sheet A. A Western design critic calls this cluttered. Conversion data in India does not agree.

The honest reading is that most of that density is load-bearing information in a market with real price sensitivity, real delivery uncertainty and a real trust deficit — a delivery date and a COD badge are not decoration, they're the deciding facts. But some of it is genuinely unmanaged accumulation: four teams each adding their strip, nobody removing.

The discussion question: given the same heuristic, how do you tell load-bearing density from accumulated clutter? One workable test: could you delete this element and would any buyer's decision change? Then make them apply it to a real page and defend each keep. That argument is worth more than the rule.

Every rewrite above does the same three things and nothing else: names the cause in the reader's language, removes the fear, and hands over a next step. If a message you've written can't be split into those three parts, it isn't finished.

The help ladder — build it top down, not bottom up

  • Rung 0 — no help needed. The label and the field explain themselves. Always try this first; every rung below is a tax.
  • Rung 1 — inline hint. One line under the field, with a real example. "Looks like yourname@bank."
  • Rung 2 — contextual help. A "what's this?" that opens next to the confusion, not on a new page that loses their form.
  • Rung 3 — searchable documentation. Task-titled, not feature-titled: "Cancel an order" beats "Order management". Concrete steps, short.
  • Rung 4 — a human. In India this is usually WhatsApp or a phone number, and that's fine — the design work is making it contextual, so the conversation opens with the order attached instead of "hi".

Most teams build rung 3 first, in a hurry, from the feature list. That's why FAQ pages answer "What is KOSA Rewards?" and never "Why was I charged twice?" Write help from your support inbox, not from your sitemap.

This is why the method survives despite everything wrong with it — the ratio of what it costs to what it catches is unmatched by anything else you can do before launch.

09 / the limits

A page can score ten out of ten and still be predatory

This is the chapter that separates a student who has memorised ten rules from a designer who understands what they measure. The heuristics are about usability. Usability is amoral — it makes whatever the interface intends easier to achieve, including things intended against the user.

First: the ten contradict each other, on purpose

TensionHow it shows up, and the usual resolution
H8 minimalism vs H1 status and H10 helpEvery status line, hint and help affordance is another element on screen. Resolution: show state and help at the moment they're needed, not permanently.
H5 prevention vs H7 efficiencyConfirmations protect novices and tax experts. Resolution: undo instead of confirm; confirm only what's irreversible.
H4 consistency vs improvementEvery genuine interface improvement violates a convention on the day it ships. Resolution: break a convention only when the gain is large and obvious, and never on the checkout.
H3 freedom vs the businessEasy exits reduce short-term conversion. Resolution: this is a real trade-off with real money on both sides — and it is where design becomes an ethical question rather than a technical one.

When two heuristics disagree, that's not a bug in the list. It's the list telling you that you've reached an actual design decision — the kind that needs a judgement, a stated reason, and preferably a measurement.

The uncomfortable finding — the best discussion question in this notebook

Take the cluttered panel from chapter 06 and audit it honestly. That countdown timer? Outstanding visibility of system status — precise, live, unambiguous. The pre-ticked shipping insurance? Excellent efficiency of use, fewer taps. "No thanks, I don't like saving money"? Perfectly clear feedback about what the button does. Fees revealed only at the last step? Each individual step is beautifully minimal.

Every one of those is a dark pattern, and not one of them is caught by the ten heuristics. Nielsen's list assumes the designer and the user want the same outcome. When they don't, usability becomes the instrument of extraction rather than the defence against it.

Ask the class: if a design scores well on all ten and still leaves the buyer worse off, what exactly were the ten measuring? There's a real answer — they measure friction, not intent — but make them arrive at it themselves.

In India this is no longer only an ethics question

The Central Consumer Protection Authority issued the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023 under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, notified on 30 November 2023. Annexure 1 specifies thirteen named dark patterns, and in June 2026 the CCPA advised e-commerce platforms to self-audit against them within three months. Your students will build for this jurisdiction.

This is not legal advice and the list evolves — the point for a design class is that in India, deceptive interface design is now a consumer-protection matter with a regulator attached, not merely a matter of taste.

The eleventh question — add it to your sheet

After the ten, ask one more, and ask it about every element you couldn't defend on usability grounds:

"If the buyer could see exactly why we put this here, would they still be glad we did?"

A delivery date survives that question. A saved address survives it. A resetting countdown does not. It isn't in Nielsen's list because Nielsen was answering a different question in 1994 — but it belongs on the sheet you hand your students in 2026.

10 / this week's exercise

Audit your own page. That's the hard part and the whole point.

Auditing someone else's site is entertainment. Auditing the page you built last week, and writing severity 4 next to something you were proud of, is the skill.

StepDoYou'll learn
1 · ScopeWrite your task in one sentence: "Buy one 30ml serum, guest checkout, UPI, on a 360px phone." Tape it above your screen.That heuristics evaluate a path, not a website.
2 · Two passesWalk it once with no notes. Walk it again with the blank sheet from chapter 08, one sheet per screen. On paper.That you see completely different things the second time.
3 · SwapTwo classmates audit your page while you audit theirs. No talking until all three are finished. Then merge the lists.Your own evaluator effect — count how many findings only one of you caught.
4 · RankSeverity-rate the merged list 0–4. Sort. Fix only the 4s and 3s this week. Leave the 1s and 2s written down and unfixed.That ranking is the deliverable, and that unfixed-but-known beats fixed-at-random.
5 · Re-auditAfter the fixes, run the same sheet again and count the new problems your fixes introduced.That every fix is also a change, and changes break things. Nobody does this step. Do it.

What "done" looks like: not a page that passes all ten — no shipped page does. A single ranked page of findings where every line names what you saw, which heuristic, a severity, and a fix a developer could start on. And at least two severity-3-or-above findings on parts of the page you designed yourself and liked. If your own page came out clean, you audited it wrong.
