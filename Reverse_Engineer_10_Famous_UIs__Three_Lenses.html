Field notes · product design · sheet 03

Reverse-engineering famous UIs — the three people looking at one screen

Earlier today you asked what a beginner, an academic and a working professional would each notice looking at the same Netflix screen. That answer was a paragraph. This is the same idea, formalised into a method, then run against ten real products — full teardowns, colour-coded by which of the three lenses is speaking. This is the Builder Stack way of studying design: you don't admire the screen, you take it apart and put it back together until you understand every decision in it.

Ten teardowns · three lenses each · logos deliberately absent

How to read this sheet

Every numbered marker on every screen is colour-coded to the lens that notices it. Same colour code, all ten teardowns — once you've internalised it here, you can run it on any app on your phone.

Red — what a beginner feels, before they can name why
Blue — what an academic names, with a citable principle
Green — what a professional sees, the metric and machinery underneath

Every screen below is a teaching reconstruction — my own CSS, built from observation, not from any company's source or screenshots. Colours and proportions are close, not exact. No logos or copyrighted marks appear anywhere in this sheet; the point is always the system, never the mark.

Method — before the teardowns
00 / the three lenses

Same screen. Three trained eyes. Three different products.

This isn't "some people notice more than others." It's that each lens is structurally blind to what the other two see — and knowing your own blind spot is most of what separates a working designer from a student.

LensWhat it noticesNative vocabularyWhere it's blind
BeginnerFriction and delight, in the moment. Reacts to the screen, doesn't interrogate it."This is confusing." "I like this." "Why did that happen?"Can't say why something works or fails — only that it does. No theory, no system.
AcademicThe named pattern underneath — which heuristic, which law, which bias is being used on them.Hick's Law, Fitts's Law, Jakob's Law, Von Restorff, Gestalt, loss aversion, anchoring.Can correctly diagnose a decision and still be wrong about why it shipped — theory doesn't know the budget, the deadline, or the metric it was built to move.
ProfessionalThe instrumented mechanism — which number this screen exists to move, and what it cost engineering to build.A/B test, conversion, retention, buy-box, dispatch, churn, north-star metric.Their own conditioning. They ship more of whatever already tested well — which is exactly why so many products, built by different companies, start to converge on the same answers.

Notice the shape of that table: each row's blind spot is basically "what the other two rows are good at." That's not a coincidence — it's why real product teams are built as trios of exactly these instincts, not one person doing all three.

What breaks if you change one thing

ChangeConsequence
Remove hero autoplayFewer "why did that start" complaints, but a real, measurable drop in discovery of new titles — this trade-off is why it hasn't been removed.
Sort rows alphabetically instead of algorithmicallyThe catalogue's depth becomes invisible again. It stops feeling personal and starts feeling like a static library.
Make the Top 10 badge brand-redIt stops reading as an objective ranking and starts reading as an ad. Trust in the signal collapses.
Ship one fixed poster per title, no personalisationClick-through drops — this is the literal, documented reason the personalised-artwork system exists.

beginner sees "Why is a bill-payment app this fancy" is the first honest reaction most people have — the interface is doing more emotional work than the task requires, and it's noticeable before it's understood. paying a credit card bill shouldn't feel like this. that's the point.
2
academic names it Access historically gated by credit score turns the product into an in-group signal — exclusivity as onboarding. 11 / the same button

Every primary CTA on this sheet is solving the identical problem. Look how differently each one answers it.

This is the closing argument of the whole exercise. If you ask "which of these ten buttons is best," the question is wrong — each one is correct for what surrounds it, and wrong for everything else.

Method — run this yourself
13 / the exercise

Rebuild, don't redesign — now with a lens attached to every note

The same five-step method from the earlier sheets, with one change: every annotation now has to declare which of the three lenses is speaking.

StepDoProduces
1 · CaptureOne real screen — a screen where a decision happens, not a home page.A constrained target.
2 · Feel itWrite down your raw, beginner reaction before you know anything. One sentence.An honest baseline, before theory contaminates it.
3 · Name itFind the citable principle behind that reaction — a real heuristic or bias, not a made-up one.Vocabulary that transfers to the next screen you look at.
4 · Follow the metricAsk what business or engineering number this decision was built to move, and what it cost to build.The part almost every student skips — and the part that separates analysis from theory.
5 · Break itChange one token. Screenshot the damage. Explain which lens notices the break first.Proof the system was understood, not just described.

Marking: a student who can only do step 2 has an opinion. A student who can do steps 2–4 has an analysis. A student who can do step 5 has understood the product well enough to have built it.

Two honest caveats to say out loud

  • These reconstructions are built from observation, not from any company's published specs, code, or screenshots. Colours and proportions are close, not exact — say so, and treat any student who later measures a real value and finds it differs as having done the exercise correctly.
  • The mechanism notes (retention curves, Buy Box scoring, artwork A/B tests) describe publicly known practices in the industry, not confirmed internal specifics of any single company's current system. Teach them as "this is how this category of decision typically gets made," not as leaked internals.
