00 One tap, traced all the way down

You open Swiggy, pick a butter chicken, and tap Place Order. A spinner appears. Roughly seven-tenths of a second later, the screen changes and says your order is confirmed. This module exists to explain every millisecond of that gap.

Not approximately. Not "the app talks to the server." Specifically: which machines were involved, what left your phone, how it got there, what happened when it arrived, what came back, and why it took the time it took.

Here is the honest framing before we start. I do not have access to Swiggy's internal architecture, and neither does anyone outside Swiggy. What follows is a reconstruction — the set of things that must happen for this feature to work at all, timed with realistic figures for an Indian 4G connection talking to servers in Mumbai. The specific milliseconds will differ. The structure will not, because the structure is forced by physics and by the problem, not by anyone's design taste.

What that picture already tells you

Six observations fall straight out of Figure 1, and each one is a section of this module.

  1. At least six separate machines were involved. Your phone, a cell tower, an internet backbone, a load balancer, an application server, a database, and Razorpay's servers — which are themselves several machines. None of them share memory. None of them can call a function on another. They can only send each other messages. This is the single most load-bearing fact in the module and it is §1.
  2. Ninety milliseconds went to setting up the connection before your request existed on the wire. That is DNS, TCP and TLS, and understanding what those three are is the difference between "the internet is slow" and knowing which part is slow. That is §2.
  3. The server re-checked the price. The phone already knew the butter chicken was ₹340. The server calculated it again anyway. Understanding why is the beginning of thinking about security, and it is in §1.
  4. The same fact now exists in four places — in your app's memory, in the server's memory, in the database, and in Razorpay's records. Keeping four copies of a fact in agreement is where most bugs in most applications actually come from. That is §4.
  5. Two things happened that nobody waited for. Notifying the restaurant and finding a delivery partner were pushed onto a queue and the response was sent without them. Knowing what can be made asynchronous is a large part of what makes apps feel fast.
  6. More than half the time was spent inside somebody else's system. You cannot make Razorpay faster. You can decide what the user sees while they wait, and that is a design decision, not an engineering one.
The claim this module makes

Everything in application development is a consequence of one constraint: your program is not one program. It is several programs, on machines you mostly do not control, connected by an unreliable channel, each holding a copy of some facts that may already be out of date.

Frameworks, languages, databases and cloud providers are all just different answers to that one problem. Learn the problem and the answers stop looking like arbitrary trivia you have to memorise.

Why we're not opening a code editor yet

The most common way beginner developers get stuck is not that a concept is hard. It is that they followed a tutorial, got a working result, and have no model of what happened — so when something breaks, they cannot form a hypothesis. They cannot even say which machine the problem is on. Debugging is hypothesis-testing, and you cannot test hypotheses you are unable to generate.

This module builds the hypothesis generator. Module 2 opens the editor.

01 The cast: what an app is actually made of

"Frontend" and "backend" are the two words beginners learn first and understand last. Here is what they actually mean, mechanically, with no metaphor.

1.1 — Two programs, not one

When you build an app, you are not writing a program. You are writing at least two separate programs that run on different computers, owned by different people, and can only communicate by sending each other text over a wire.

Read that again, because almost every beginner mistake is a violation of it.

The client — runs on the user's device

Where: the phone in someone's hand, in Bilaspur or Bangalore. You will never see this machine.

Who controls it: the user, and by extension anyone who can get hold of the device. Not you.

What it can do: draw the screen, handle touch, store a small amount of data locally, and send messages.

What it knows: only what you sent it, and only as of when you sent it.

The server — runs on a machine you rent

Where: a data centre. For Indian products, usually AWS Mumbai, Google Cloud Mumbai/Delhi, or Azure Central India.

Who controls it: you. Nobody else can read its memory or change its code.

What it can do: talk to the database, hold secrets, decide what is true, talk to other companies' servers.

What it knows: everything, and it is the only thing entitled to say so.

Every other piece of vocabulary you will meet is a specialisation of one of these two, or a service that sits between them. Figure 2 is the full cast.

The vocabulary, decoded

Frontend = the client. Code that runs on the user's device (or in their browser). Backend = everything in the green column: the API server, the database, the workers. Full-stack = someone who works on both. API = the specific list of messages your backend agrees to accept and answer — nothing more mystical than that. The cloud = someone else's computers, rented by the hour.

1.2 — What runs where, and why it matters

When something breaks — and things break constantly — the first and most useful question is always which machine is this happening on? Beginners who cannot answer that question are reduced to changing things at random.

1.3 — The three things a client can never be trusted with

This is the most important paragraph in §1, and it is the one that separates people who build toys from people who build products.

Your app is not yours once it ships. It sits on a device the user controls. They can read its files, inspect its network traffic with free tools, modify its behaviour, and send your server any message they like — including messages your app would never send. Not because your users are attackers, but because some users of any product with money in it will be.

You will get this wrong

The bug: your checkout request contains "total": 340, because it was convenient — the app already calculated it for the screen, so why compute it twice?

How it presents: it works perfectly. For months. Then one order arrives for ₹1. Nothing in your logs looks wrong, because from the server's point of view nothing was wrong — it was told ₹1 and it believed it.

The fix is structural, not defensive: don't add a check that the total looks reasonable. Stop accepting the total. Accept item_id and quantity; compute the rest. A field the server never reads cannot be tampered with.

The request body contains identifiers and intent only. No prices, no permissions, no totals, no user roles.

1.4 — Why the split exists at all

A fair beginner question: if the split causes this much trouble, why not put everything on the device? Four reasons, and they are worth being able to state.

1 · Shared state

The restaurant's remaining stock is one fact that thousands of phones need to agree about. It cannot live on any one of them. Anything shared needs a single place to live, and that place is the server.

2 · Trust

§1.3. Some decisions must be made somewhere the user cannot reach. Money, permissions, and anything with a rule attached.

3 · Durability

Phones are lost, wiped, replaced and run out of storage. Your users' data has to survive that. A server with backups survives it; a phone does not.

4 · Change without shipping

You can change what the server says today. Changing what the app does requires a new build, a store review, and users choosing to update — which many never will. §6 covers the real cost of this.

The useful corollary, and it is genuinely liberating for a first project: if none of those four apply, you do not need a backend at all. A unit converter, an offline note-taking tool, a calculator, a timer — these are complete products with no server whatsoever, and they are excellent first builds precisely because they remove five of Figure 2's boxes.

Checkpoint — where does the logic go?

Question: You're building an app for a Dehradun gym. A member scans a QR code at the door to check in. The app should refuse check-in if their membership has expired. Where does the expiry check happen, and why?

Answer: Both places, doing different jobs. The app checks so it can grey out the button and say "your membership expired on 3 March — renew?" — that is UX, and it makes the honest case fast and pleasant. The server checks because that is the actual rule, and it must, for three separate reasons: the app's copy of the expiry date may be stale (it was fetched at last login); anyone can send the check-in request directly without using the app; and the expiry date is a conclusion, which by §1.3 the client is never allowed to assert. Note the general shape: client-side checks are a courtesy to honest users, server-side checks are the rule. Removing the client check makes the app annoying. Removing the server check makes it broken.

Teaching note — the demonstration that makes this permanent

Open any e-commerce site in a desktop browser, open developer tools, go to the Network tab, and add something to the cart. Show the students the actual request going out — the method, the path, the JSON body. Then right-click it and choose "Copy as cURL," paste it into a terminal, edit one number, and run it.

The moment a student sees a request go to a real server without a browser form being involved, "the client can't be trusted" stops being a slogan. It takes four minutes and it lands harder than any slide. (Use a site you own or a public API sandbox — the point is the mechanism, and there's no need to poke at someone else's production system to make it.)

Two programs. Different machines. Messages only. Everything else follows.

Secrets, validation, truth. The client gets none of the three.

Send identifiers and intent. Never send conclusions.

"Which machine is this happening on?" — the first debugging question, always.

02 How a message physically gets there

Rows 3, 4 and 5 of Figure 1 consumed 90 milliseconds before your request existed. This section is what those rows were doing — and why knowing it changes how you design, not just how you debug.

2.1 — Two kinds of address

Machines on the internet are found by IP address — a number, like 13.234.176.102. Humans use domain names, like api.swiggy.com. Both exist because they solve different problems: numbers are what routing hardware can act on, names are what people can remember and what companies can move.

That second part matters more than it sounds. A domain name is a layer of indirection, and indirection is what lets a company replace every server it owns without anyone changing anything. The name stays; what it points to changes. This is also why the name has to be looked up every time — and that lookup is called DNS.

2.2 — DNS: turning a name into a number

Before any planned domain change, lower the TTL to 300 seconds at least a day in advance, then change, then raise it back.

2.3 — IP, TCP and TLS: three layers doing three jobs

Once you have the number, three separate systems cooperate to get a message there and back. They are usually taught as an abstract stack; here is what each one is actually for.

LayerIts one jobWhat it does NOT promiseCost to you
IPMove a packet of bytes from one address to another, by whatever route works.That it arrives. That packets arrive in order. That it arrives once. IP is explicitly best-effort.Nothing — it's the substrate
TCPMake an unreliable packet service look like a reliable, ordered stream of bytes.Speed. TCP achieves reliability by acknowledging and retransmitting, which costs round trips.1 round trip to open a connection
TLSMake that stream private and verify you're talking to who you think you are.That the other end is honest — only that it is who it claims to be. A verified certificate proves identity, not integrity.1 more round trip (TLS 1.3)

HTTPS is not a separate protocol. It is ordinary HTTP running inside a TLS-encrypted TCP connection. That is the whole of it. The padlock in a browser means the connection is encrypted and the certificate matches the domain — it makes no claim whatsoever about whether the site is trustworthy. protocol definition

2.4 — Latency is physics, and you cannot code around it

This is the part beginners most often refuse to believe, so here is the arithmetic.

Light in a vacuum travels 299,792 km per second. In the glass of a fibre-optic cable it travels at roughly two-thirds of that — about 200,000 km/s. That is a hard ceiling set by the refractive index of glass, and no cloud provider, framework or optimisation will move it. physical constant

The design consequences, stated plainly
  • Host in India if your users are in India. Choosing us-east-1 because a tutorial did adds ~200 ms to every single request, forever. This is the highest-leverage decision in this entire module and it takes one dropdown.
  • Count round trips, not kilobytes. A screen that makes six sequential requests costs six latencies. Make it one request that returns everything, or make the six run in parallel.
  • Put static files on a CDN. Images, fonts and video get copied to servers near the user. The green bar shrinks because the distance shrinks.
  • Design for the wait you cannot remove. If 240 ms is unavoidable, the question stops being "how do I make it faster" and becomes "what does the user see during it" — which is a UX decision, covered in the companion series.

Run it a second time immediately. DNS drops to near zero, because of Figure 4. Run it against an Indian domain and a US domain and compare. This is the whole of §2, made real, in about four minutes.

You will get this wrong

The mistake: building and testing entirely on your laptop, on office Wi-Fi, against a server running on localhost. Every request takes under a millisecond, because it never leaves the machine. Nothing in Figure 1 rows 3–6 exists.

How it presents: the app is beautiful in the demo and unusable in the field. Loading states were never designed because loading was never visible. Timeouts were never set because nothing ever timed out. Two requests were made sequentially because it made no measurable difference.

The habit that prevents it: from your very first working screen, test on a real phone, on mobile data, with the laptop's network throttled. Chrome DevTools and Xcode both have a "slow 3G" setting. Ten seconds to enable, and it changes what you build.

Every network call has an explicit timeout. The default in most libraries is "wait forever," which is how spinners become permanent.
Checkpoint — diagnose from the numbers

Question: A user in Chennai reports that your app takes about 4 seconds to load its home screen. You run the curl command against your API and see: DNS 0.02s, TCP 0.06s, TLS 0.11s, first byte 3.9s, total 3.9s. Where is the problem, and where is it definitely not?

Answer: It is definitely not the network and definitely not DNS. Everything up to TLS completed in 110 ms, which is a healthy connection. The 3.8-second gap between TLS completing and the first byte arriving is the server thinking — your backend code, or a database query it is waiting on, or a third-party call it made. That is where to look, and it means the fix is in your code rather than in your hosting.

Note what the measurement bought you: without it, "the app is slow" has six plausible causes and you would probably have started by changing the wrong one. With it, four of the six are eliminated in ten seconds. Measure before you optimise is not a productivity slogan; it is how you avoid spending a week on the network when the problem was a missing database index.

Teaching note — running Lab 1 in class

This lab works better as a live race than as an exercise. Have every student run ping against one Indian domain and one US domain simultaneously and call out their numbers. The spread across the room — different networks, different ISPs, someone on hostel Wi-Fi getting 400 ms — is itself the lesson, and it is more convincing than any figure you can put on a slide.

Windows students will find ping -c 5 fails; the flag is -n 5 on Windows. Put both forms on the board before you start or you will spend fifteen minutes on it.

If your institution's network blocks ICMP (some do), fall back to the curl command, which uses ordinary HTTPS and gets through.

DNS = name → number. It's a chain of caches, and TTL decides how stale they're allowed to be.

TCP makes it reliable. TLS makes it private. Each costs one round trip.

The unit of cost is a round trip, not a byte. Fewer, bigger requests beat many small ones.

Host in India for Indian users. One dropdown, 200 ms per request, forever.

03 The message itself: HTTP, JSON, and what an API really is

§2 got a message to the server. This section is what was written on it. There is less magic here than anyone expects — an HTTP request is a short piece of text with a strict layout, and you can write one by hand.

3.1 — An HTTP request is four things

Every request, from every app, on every platform, to every server on the internet, consists of exactly four parts. Once you can name them, "the API isn't working" turns into "the third part is wrong," which is a fixable statement.

Why idempotency is a §0 idea, not an advanced one

Go back to Figure 1. Row 11 was a payment that took 345 ms inside somebody else's system. Now imagine the phone lost signal at millisecond 400. The user's money moved. Their app says the order failed. What happens next is entirely determined by whether you thought about this before writing the code.

The rule: any request that changes something and might be retried needs an idempotency key. In practice that means every payment, every order, every "submit," every transfer. Razorpay, Stripe and every serious payment provider require or strongly recommend one, which tells you how common the failure is. vendor documentation

3.2 — The methods, and what they promise

MethodMeansSafe to retry?Beginner mistake
GETGive me this thing. Change nothing.Yes, alwaysUsing GET for an action ("/deleteOrder?id=4"). Browsers, proxies and prefetchers will call GETs on their own. People have deleted their own databases this way.
POSTCreate something new, or perform an action.No — unless you add an idempotency keyAssuming a failed POST didn't happen. It very often did.
PUTReplace this thing entirely with what I'm sending.Yes — sending it twice gives the same resultUsing PUT for a partial update, which silently wipes the fields you didn't send.
PATCHChange only the fields I'm sending.UsuallyNothing serious. This is the one you want for edit screens.
DELETERemove this thing.Yes — it's already gone the second timeActually deleting. In most products you want a deleted_at timestamp instead, because users ask for things back.

"Safe to retry" is the column that matters, and it has a proper name: idempotent means doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. GET, PUT and DELETE are idempotent by definition. POST is not, which is the entire reason the header in Figure 7 exists.

3.3 — Status codes: the server's one-word answer

Every response opens with a three-digit number. The first digit is the whole story; the other two are detail.

You will get this wrong

The mistake: returning 200 OK for everything, with the real outcome buried in the body — {"success": false, "error": "out of stock"}.

Why it seems fine: your own app handles it, because you wrote both ends.

Why it isn't: everything between the two ends is now blind. Your monitoring reports 100% success while users see failures. Caches store errors as if they were valid answers. Retry logic never fires because nothing looks retryable. Load balancers keep sending traffic to a broken server. The status code is not a message to your app — it is a message to every piece of infrastructure in between, and there is more of that than you think.

3.4 — JSON, and why it won

The body needs a format both ends agree on. JSON — JavaScript Object Notation — is that format for essentially all modern APIs. It has exactly six types and no more:

jsonthe entire specification, in one example
{
  "order_id": 8412,                          // number — no separate int/float
  "restaurant": "Bikanervala, Rajouri Garden", // string — always double quotes
  "is_paid": true,                          // boolean
  "delivered_at": null,                     // null — "we know it's empty"
  "items": [                                // array — ordered list
    { "name": "Dal Makhani", "qty": 2, "price_paise": 34000 },
    { "name": "Butter Naan", "qty": 4, "price_paise": 6000 }
  ],
  "address": {                              // object — nested key/value
    "line1": "B-14, Sector 62",
    "city": "Noida",
    "pincode": "201309"                    // string, not number — see below
  }
}
Two decisions in there that will save you real pain →

Money is stored in paise, as an integer. 34000, not 340.00. Decimal numbers in most programming languages are binary floating point, which cannot represent 0.1 exactly — so 0.1 + 0.2 gives 0.30000000000000004 in JavaScript, Python, Java and almost everywhere else. IEEE 754, universal Add up a few thousand orders and your totals drift by rupees. Store the smallest unit as a whole number and divide only when displaying. Every payment system does this; Razorpay's API takes amounts in paise for exactly this reason.

A pincode is a string, not a number. So is a phone number, an OTP, an Aadhaar-style identifier and an order reference. The test is simple: would you ever do arithmetic on it? Nobody adds two pincodes. Store it as a number and 0110xx becomes 110xx the first time it round-trips, because leading zeros are not a thing numbers have.

Money is an integer in the smallest unit. Identifiers are strings. Both decisions are almost impossible to reverse once you have real data.

3.5 — So what actually is an API?

Strip away the mystique: an API is a written promise about which messages you will accept and what you will send back. That is the entire concept. It is a contract, not a technology.

A small but complete API for a tiffin service:

Method + pathMeansReturns
GET /api/v1/menuWhat can I order today?200 + array of items
GET /api/v1/menu/dal_makhaniDetails of one item200 + object, or 404
POST /api/v1/ordersPlace an order201 + the new order, or 422
GET /api/v1/orders/8412How's my order doing?200, or 403 if it isn't yours
PATCH /api/v1/orders/8412Change the delivery slot200, or 409 if it already shipped
DELETE /api/v1/orders/8412Cancel it204, or 409 if too late

Three conventions are doing quiet work there, and they are worth adopting on day one:

  • Paths name things, not actions. /orders is a noun. The verb lives in the method. You never need /createOrder, /getOrder and /cancelOrder — that is three addresses doing the work of one. This convention is called REST, and this is 90% of what the word means in practice.
  • Version from the first line of code. /api/v1/ costs you nothing today. Without it, the day you need to change a response shape, you must either break every app already installed on people's phones or invent a versioning scheme under pressure. Old app versions live on devices for years — §6 explains why.
  • Plural collections, singular members. /orders is the list; /orders/8412 is one of them. Trivial, but consistency here is the difference between an API a stranger can guess and one they must constantly look up.

Four things just happened that are worth naming, because each is a section of this module made concrete:

  1. You sent an HTTP request without an app. The four parts of Figure 7 were all there; curl just filled in the boring ones.
  2. You received JSON and it was, as promised, plain text with six types in it.
  3. You got a 404 and a 401, and you can now say precisely what each one means and what an app should do about it.
  4. You discovered rate limiting — x-ratelimit-remaining. Every serious API counts your requests and will start returning 429 when you exceed the allowance. This is why an app that fetches on every keystroke gets banned, and why you will meet the word "debounce" in Module 2.
The reframe worth carrying forward

Everything you will build in Modules 2 and 3 is a nicer way of doing what you just did with curl. React Native's fetch builds the same four-part message. Razorpay's SDK builds the same four-part message. Your own backend will read the same four-part message. There is no layer underneath this that you haven't now seen.

Checkpoint — design the contract

Question: A user wants to change the delivery address on an order that has already been dispatched. What method, what path, and what status code — and what should the app show?

Answer: PATCH /api/v1/orders/8412 with {"address_id": 4472}, and the server returns 409 Conflict. PATCH because it is a partial update of an existing thing (PUT would require resending the whole order and would wipe anything omitted). 409 rather than 400, because the request was perfectly well-formed — it conflicts with the current state of the resource, which is a different problem and deserves a different code. Not 403, because the user is entitled to edit their own order; they just can't edit this one now.

The app should show what changed and what to do instead: "This order is already on its way, so the address is locked. Call the rider?" — with the rider's number. Note that the status code told the app enough to produce a useful screen. That is the payoff for choosing codes carefully: a well-chosen status code is a design asset, because the client can branch on it without parsing prose.

Teaching note — the 20-minute version of §3

If time is short, cut §3.2's full method table and teach only GET and POST, then spend the recovered time on Lab 2. Students who have personally received a 401 and a 404 from a real server retain the status-code families; students who have only seen the table do not.

Have them run the last command in Lab 2 and predict the code before pressing enter. Most guess 403. The discussion about why it is 401 is worth more than the ten minutes it takes, because the 401/403 distinction is the single most common API design error in student projects.

For non-technical cohorts, Postman or Hoppscotch give the same lesson with a graphical interface and no terminal anxiety. The concepts are identical; only the typing changes.

04 State: the hard part nobody warns beginners about

Ask an experienced engineer where the bugs are and they will not say "algorithms." They will say state. This section explains why, and it is the most valuable thing in this module.

4.1 — What state actually is

State is any fact your program remembers between one moment and the next. Whether a spinner is showing. What the user typed. Which items are in the cart. Whether they are logged in. How many portions of dal makhani are left.

A program with no state is a calculator: input goes in, output comes out, nothing is remembered. Those programs are easy and nobody pays for them. Every product you have ever used is mostly state management with a thin layer of screens on top.

The trouble begins with a fact that seems too obvious to state: the same fact usually exists in more than one place at the same time, and each copy has its own idea of when it was last correct.

4.2 — The seven places one fact can live

4.3 — Why two copies always diverge

Here is the mechanism, with two users and one portion of dal makhani.

The general principle

Displayed data is always a photograph, never a live feed. By the time a number is on a screen, it describes the past. On a fast connection the photograph is a few hundred milliseconds old; on a train in Uttarakhand it may be four minutes old.

This is why professional apps re-validate at the point of commitment rather than at the point of display, and why 409 Conflict exists as a status code at all. The correct question is never "how do I keep the screen perfectly in sync?" — that is impossible. It is "what should happen when the user acts on something that has changed?"

Every action that depends on shared data re-checks on the server, inside a transaction, at the moment of commitment.

4.4 — Optimistic updates: making slow things feel instant

Go back to Figure 1. When you tapped the heart on a restaurant, did you wait 640 ms to see it turn red? No — it turned red immediately. That is an optimistic update: the app changes the screen as if the request had already succeeded, then sends it, then quietly corrects itself if it was wrong.

You will get this wrong

The mistake: building the success path only. In development your server is on localhost and it never fails, so the error branch is never exercised — sometimes never even written.

How it presents: in production, roughly 1–3% of requests fail for ordinary reasons — a tunnel, a lift, a dropped 4G handover, a server restarting during deploy. Those users get option B. They don't file a bug; they just quietly stop trusting the app.

The habit: whenever you write a request, write the failure branch in the same sitting, before you move on. Then test it by turning off Wi-Fi mid-tap. If you cannot easily test failure, that is itself the signal that your code has no failure path.

4.5 — Staleness: how old is too old?

Every cached copy needs a policy. There are only four, and choosing between them is a product decision more than a technical one.

StrategyHow it worksRight forCost
Fetch every timeNo cache. Ask the server whenever the screen appears.Bank balance, order status, seat availabilityA full round trip on every view. Blank screen on bad networks.
Stale-while-revalidateShow the cached copy instantly, fetch fresh in the background, update quietly when it arrives.Almost everything — menus, feeds, profiles, listsThe user briefly sees old data. Usually invisible and always better than a spinner.
Time-to-liveCache is valid for N seconds, then refetched.Search results, category listings, prices that change hourlyYou must pick N, and N is always wrong somewhere.
Invalidate on writeWhen something changes, explicitly throw away the caches that depend on it.The user's own data, right after they edit itYou must remember every dependent cache. Missing one is the classic "I saved it but it still shows the old value" bug.

Stale-while-revalidate is the default you want, and it is worth understanding why in terms of §2: showing something instantly costs zero round trips, and the refresh happens on a connection the user is not waiting on. It converts a 240 ms blank screen into a 0 ms populated screen that quietly improves. It is the single biggest perceived-performance win available to a beginner, and it costs almost nothing to implement.

Why "cache invalidation" is the famous joke

There is an old line among programmers that there are only two hard problems in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things. It is funny because it is a genuine observation. Caching is easy; knowing precisely when a cached copy stopped being true requires understanding every path through your system that could have changed it. Miss one path, and one screen somewhere shows a number from twenty minutes ago with total confidence.

The practical defence for a first product: cache less than you think you need to, and always give the user a way to force a refresh — pull-to-refresh exists partly for this reason.

4.6 — The seam bug: when two systems disagree and neither is wrong

This is the hardest class of bug in application development, and it deserves naming before you meet it in the wild.

Your server calls Razorpay. Razorpay takes ₹340. Razorpay's response begins its journey back to your server — and your server crashes, or the connection drops, or the process is killed by a deploy. Money has moved. Your database does not know.

Note carefully what is not the problem. Nobody wrote a bug. Both systems behaved correctly. Razorpay took the payment it was asked for; your server recorded nothing because it never learned anything to record. The failure lives in the seam between them, and the seam is a place neither party controls.

Why you can't just use a transaction

Databases give you atomicity — either all the writes happen or none do. That guarantee stops at the edge of your database. You cannot wrap "charge Razorpay" and "insert order row" in one transaction, because one of them is a different company's computer.

Defence 1 — webhooks

Razorpay also sends you an independent message when a payment settles. If your server missed the response, the webhook arrives later and you reconcile. This is why webhooks exist, and why they must be idempotent — you will receive some of them twice.

Defence 2 — write intent first

Create the order row as pending before calling Razorpay. Now a crash leaves you with a record you can investigate, rather than silence. A pending order that never resolved is a bug you can find; a payment with no record is one you cannot.

The pattern, in four steps
  1. Write your intent to your own database first, marked pending, with the idempotency key.
  2. Call the third party.
  3. Update your record with the outcome.
  4. Have a background job that finds records stuck in pending for more than N minutes, asks the third party what actually happened, and resolves them.

Step 4 is the one everyone skips, and it is the one that makes the other three worth doing. Module 3 builds this properly; for now, the point is that this problem is structural and every payments system on earth has this exact code in it.

4.7 — Offline: the outbox

Section 7 of the companion UX series established that in India, intermittent connectivity is the normal case rather than the exception. The state consequence is a pattern worth knowing by name: the outbox.

When the user acts and there is no network, you do not show an error. You write the action to a local queue on the device, show it as done (or as pending), and send it when connectivity returns. WhatsApp's clock-then-tick icon is exactly this, made visible: the message exists locally, has not yet reached the server, and will.

Action typeOutbox appropriate?Because
Send a message, save a note, mark a task doneYes — this is the pattern's home groundNobody else is competing for the same resource. Order of arrival barely matters.
Like, follow, bookmarkYesReversible, low stakes, and the user's intent is unambiguous.
Place an order against limited stockNo§4.3 — the item may be gone. Queuing it promises something you cannot deliver.
Any paymentNeverYou cannot know the outcome, and a queued payment that fires four hours later on a metro platform is worse than one that never fired.
Every screen defines what it does with no network: cached-and-marked-stale, queued via outbox, or an explicit, honest failure. "Infinite spinner" is not one of the three.
Checkpoint — trace the state

Question: A user edits their delivery address in your app. They tap Save, see a success toast, then navigate back to the order screen — which still shows the old address. Nothing crashed. Where is the bug, and which of the seven locations in Figure 9 is involved?

Answer: The write succeeded — the database (location 6) has the new address. The bug is that at least one cached copy was not invalidated: most likely the app-wide store (location 2), which is still holding the address object it fetched when the order screen first loaded, and possibly the on-device cache (location 3) as well. The order screen re-rendered from memory without refetching, because from its point of view nothing changed.

This is the "invalidate on write" row of §4.5, and it is the most common state bug in a first app. Two fixes exist and they differ in philosophy: explicitly invalidate the address cache when a save succeeds (precise, and you will eventually forget one), or adopt stale-while-revalidate so every screen refetches on appearance (slightly more traffic, far fewer bugs). For a first product, choose the second. Prefer the strategy that fails safely over the one that is theoretically optimal.

Avoid teaching this with code. State is a reasoning skill, and code obscures it at this stage. Module 2 will supply the syntax once the model is in place.

If you have only one hour for §4, teach §4.2, §4.3 and §4.4 and drop the rest. Those three cover the failures students will actually hit in a capstone project.

05 The build-target decision

"Should I build native or cross-platform?" is the first real decision in an app project, it is made once, and reversing it later costs months. Here is how to make it on evidence rather than on whichever tutorial you watched first.

5.1 — What each option actually is, mechanically

The marketing descriptions of these options are useless because they all claim to do the same thing. The mechanical descriptions are useful, because they explain exactly where each one is fast, where it is slow, and what it can and cannot reach.

5.2 — The decision criteria

Score your project against these eight rows. Any row where you answer "strongly yes" in the right-hand column should push you toward native regardless of the others.

CriterionPoints to cross-platformPoints to native
Team skillsYou or your team know JavaScript / React. This is the most common situation and the strongest single argument.You already have iOS or Android engineers, or you're building for one platform only.
Budget and timelineOne codebase, one team, one QA pass. Realistically 50–65% of the cost of two native apps. my estimate from project experienceYou have the budget for two teams and the deadline to match.

5.3 — The default I would actually pick, and what would change my mind

Stated default

For a first mobile product built by someone coming from web, with no unusual hardware requirements and a need to reach both platforms: React Native, using Expo. engineering judgement, not a universal truth

Three specific reasons, in order of weight:

  • Your existing knowledge transfers almost completely. JavaScript, components, props, state, npm — you already have all of it. The genuinely new material is layout (flexbox without CSS), navigation and native modules. That is weeks, not months.
  • Expo removes the setup cliff. Historically the hardest part of mobile development for a beginner was Xcode, Android Studio, SDK versions, provisioning profiles and Gradle — a wall that stops a large fraction of people before they render anything. Expo lets you run on your own physical phone in minutes with a QR code. That is not a minor convenience; it is the difference between finishing and quitting.
  • You keep the escape hatches. If you eventually need genuine native code, React Native lets you write it and call it. You are not trapped, which is the fear that makes people over-think this decision.

What would change my mind, specifically:

  • Heavy custom animation or graphics → Flutter, or native. Flutter's own rendering engine is a real advantage when you are drawing something unusual sixty times a second.
  • One platform only, and it's iOS → native Swift with SwiftUI. Cross-platform's entire value proposition is the second platform. If there isn't one, you are paying a tax for nothing.
  • Content with no device APIs — a news reader, a course catalogue, a documentation site → a responsive web app or PWA. Do not build an app for something that is a website. Users will not install it, and you have taken on store review and update latency for no benefit.
  • The team already knows Dart → Flutter, without hesitation. Existing team skill beats every argument in §5.2.
  • Your first users are on ₹8,000 Android phones and install size is the constraint → this is the strongest argument for native Android or a PWA, and §6.4 explains why.
You will get this wrong

The mistake: choosing the stack by GitHub stars, YouTube view counts, or which one a founder you admire mentioned on a podcast.

Why it's tempting: the criteria in §5.2 require you to know things about your project you don't know yet, whereas popularity is a number you can look up in ten seconds.

The correction: only two of the eight rows genuinely matter for a first project — what your team already knows and what device APIs you need. Answer those two honestly and the decision usually makes itself. Everything else is optimisation for a scale you do not have yet, and you can revisit it when you do.

The build-target decision is written down, with the two or three criteria that drove it. Future you will ask, and "it seemed popular" is not an answer you can defend.

The question isn't "which is best." It's "what draws the button" — everything follows from that.

Default: React Native + Expo, if you're coming from web.

Don't build an app for something that is a website.

06 What actually ships

You have written some files. There is an app on a phone in Bilaspur. This section is every step in between — and the one property of that journey that will shape your API design for years.

6.1 — From your folder to their phone

6.2 — The property that changes your API design

Here is the fact that separates web thinking from app thinking, and it is worth more than any framework knowledge in this module.

On the web

You deploy. Every user gets the new version on their next page load — seconds later. There is exactly one version of your code in existence at any moment.

If you make a breaking change, you change both sides simultaneously and nobody notices.

In an app

You submit. It's reviewed. It's released. Then each user updates whenever they feel like it — and a meaningful share never do, because auto-update is off, or storage is full, or their phone stopped receiving OS updates.

Every version you have ever shipped is still running on someone's phone right now, and it is talking to your server.

Every API change is additive. Adding a field is free; removing or renaming one breaks every app version already installed.

6.3 — What "the bundle" contains, and why size is a business metric

The file you upload contains your compiled code, every image and font you shipped, every library you installed (including the ones you installed and stopped using), and metadata. On the Indian device floor established in the companion UX series, its size is not a technical detail — it decides whether the install completes.

What's insideTypical shareHow it gets out of hand
Your own codeSmall — often under 10%Rarely the problem. Beginners assume it is and optimise the wrong thing.
Framework runtimeFixed cost: Flutter's engine ~4–8 MB, React Native's runtime similar order of magnitudeUnavoidable, and part of the §5 decision.
Libraries you installedFrequently the largest sliceOne date-formatting library with every locale in the world. One icon set where you used four icons.
Images and fontsOften 30–60%Uncompressed PNGs at @3x. Six font weights where you use two. An onboarding video.

The target is set by users with 3 GB of free storage who are deciding between your app and their photographs — and the existence of "Lite" builds across Indian consumer apps is the market's answer to that constraint.

The reframe

Install size is a conversion metric, not an engineering metric. Every megabyte is a small tax on the probability that a download started on 4G actually finishes. Treat it the way you treat page weight on the web: measure it, budget it, and know which decision made it grow.

Checkpoint — the breaking change

Question: Your API returns {"price": 340} in rupees. You realise §3.4 was right and you should be using paise. You change the server to return {"price": 34000}. What happens, and what should you have done?

Answer: Every installed copy of your app now displays ₹34,000 for a ₹340 item, and keeps doing so until each user individually updates. Some never will. You have not introduced a display bug — you have broken a contract that thousands of already-shipped clients depend on.

The correct move is additive: keep price returning rupees exactly as before, and add price_paise. New app versions read the new field; old versions keep working forever. Once your analytics show old versions have fallen below whatever share you're willing to abandon, you may remove the old field — or, more often, you simply never do, because a dead field costs nothing and removing it costs a support incident.

The general rule, and it is worth memorising: in mobile, you add and deprecate. You do not change and remove.

Teaching note — the exercise that makes §6.2 stick

Ask students to open their phone's app store, go to the update list, and count how many apps are waiting to be updated. Then ask how long some of them have been waiting. The room's own phones are the evidence, and it converts an abstract curve into "oh — I am the red line."

Follow with: "your app is on that list. Design as if the user will never tap Update." Students who internalise this write better APIs immediately, and it costs one minute of class time.

Signing key: back it up on day one. Losing it ends the product.

Every version you ever shipped is still running somewhere, calling your API.

Add fields. Never rename or remove them.

8.1 — The one question, then the tree

Debugging is not a talent. It is a search procedure: form a hypothesis about which component is misbehaving, find the cheapest test that eliminates half the possibilities, repeat. People who look fast at it are not thinking faster — they are eliminating more per step.

Teaching note — turning the studio into assessment

Exercise 1 marks itself and is excellent pre-class homework: twelve students bring five requests each and you have sixty real examples from Indian products to teach §3 from. Far better than invented ones.

Exercise 2 is the assessable artefact. Mark it on three things and nothing else: can they justify every box, did they draw the failure paths, and did they cross anything out. The third is the strongest predictor of whether a student's capstone will ship. A diagram with nothing crossed out is a student who has not yet made a decision.

Exercise 3 is the one students resist and the one that pays off most in the capstone. If you can only run two, run 1 and 3 — the architecture skill develops naturally during building, but the state discipline does not.

10 Glossary, sources, and what Module 2 covers

10.1 — Everything this module introduced

TermMeaning, in one sentence
APIA written promise about which messages a server accepts and what it sends back. A contract, not a technology.
BackendEverything that runs on machines you control: the API server, the database, the workers.
BundleYour source code merged, minified and packaged into the single artefact that ships to a device.
CacheA fast copy of something slow to obtain — and therefore a copy that can be wrong.
CDNA network of servers holding copies of your static files close to users, so the physical distance in §2.4 shrinks.
ClientThe program running on the user's device. Untrusted by definition.
Code signingThe cryptographic signature that proves an update came from you. Lose the key and you cannot update your own app.
Compiled / interpreted / transpiledTranslated ahead of time to machine code / executed line by line at runtime / translated to another source language.
DNSThe system that turns a domain name into an IP address, via a chain of caches governed by TTL.
Deep linkA URL that opens a specific screen inside an app rather than its home screen.
Environment variableA value read at startup from outside the source code — how secrets stay out of git and how one codebase becomes many deployments.
FrontendThe client. Code that runs on the user's device or in their browser.
HTTPThe text format every request and response uses: method and path, headers, blank line, body.
HTTPSHTTP inside a TLS-encrypted connection. Not a separate protocol.
IdempotentDoing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. GET, PUT and DELETE are; POST is not, which is why idempotency keys exist.
IP addressThe numeric address of a machine on a network.
JSONThe text format almost all APIs use for message bodies. Six types: string, number, boolean, null, array, object.
LatencyHow long a round trip takes. Governed by distance and the speed of light in glass; unrelated to bandwidth.
Load balancerThe machine that spreads incoming requests across several identical servers.
Optimistic updateChanging the screen as if a request had already succeeded, then rolling back if it didn't.
OutboxA local queue of user actions taken while offline, sent when connectivity returns.
PWAA website with a manifest that can be added to a home screen — no store, no review, no install.
Queue + workerA list of jobs nobody is waiting for, and the program that works through them.
Rate limitingA server counting your requests and returning 429 past an allowance.
Reconciliation jobA background task that finds records stuck in an unresolved state and asks a third party what actually happened.
RESTThe convention that paths name things (nouns) and HTTP methods supply the verbs.
Round trip / RTTOne message out and one back. The real unit of cost on a network.
ServerA program running on a machine you control. The only place decisions about truth may be made.
Source of truthThe one location whose copy of a fact is authoritative. Everything else is a cache.
Stale-while-revalidateShow the cached copy instantly, fetch fresh in the background, update quietly. The default worth adopting.
StateAny fact your program remembers between one moment and the next. Where the bugs are.
Status codeThe three-digit answer opening every response. First digit: 2 worked, 3 elsewhere, 4 your fault, 5 theirs.
TCPThe layer that makes an unreliable packet service look like a reliable ordered stream. Costs one round trip to open.
TLSThe layer that encrypts the stream and verifies the server's identity. Costs one more round trip.
TTLTime to live — how long a cached answer (DNS or otherwise) may be reused before it must be refetched.
WebhookA message a third party sends you unprompted when something happens on their side. Must be idempotent, because you will receive some twice.

10.2 — On the numbers in this module

Three classes of claim appear in this document, and they are labelled differently on purpose. Please preserve the distinction if you teach from it, because students quote everything with equal confidence.

Documented

Protocol behaviour, HTTP status code meanings, JSON's type list, IEEE 754 floating point, the speed of light. These are specifications and physical constants. They will not change.

Dated

Store fees, review timelines, tester requirements, size limits, hosting costs, framework runtime sizes. All checked in early 2026, all subject to change without notice. Verify before planning around any of them.

Judgement

The React Native recommendation, the cost estimates, the "50–65% of two native apps" figure, the ordering of the decision criteria. These are my views from having done this. Reasonable people differ.

The latency figures in Figure 6 and the waterfall in Figure 1 are order-of-magnitude reconstructions, not measurements of any specific company's system. They are correct in shape and proportion, which is what matters for the argument; the exact milliseconds on your network will differ, sometimes by a lot. §2.5 shows you how to measure your own, and you should.

One last thing before you close this

You now know more about how applications work than most people who have shipped one. That is not flattery — it is a comment on how the subject is usually taught. Most people learn a framework and reverse-engineer the model over years of confusing bugs.

The thing that will make this stick is not re-reading it. It is Exercise 2. Draw your own system, on paper, with the failure paths in red. An hour of that will do more than another four hours of reading, because it forces you to make decisions rather than recognise them.

Then open the editor.

One fact, five consequences, and everything else follows.

Do Exercise 2. Paper, marker, red pen. It's the one that changes things.
