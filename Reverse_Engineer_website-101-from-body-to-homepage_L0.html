Field notes · absolute beginners · sheet 00

From <body> to homepage

You've seen the boilerplate. Blank <head>, empty <body>. This notebook reverse-engineers a real kind of website — an Indian D2C store, built page by page — and points at every single box saying this is that tag, arranged this way. Nothing here requires knowing what "design" means yet. It only requires the boilerplate you already have.

Every screen below is HTML + CSS. No magic.

One honest note before we start

The site in this notebook — KOSA — is not real. It's built to look like the dozens of Indian skincare/D2C sites you've already used (Mamaearth, Nykaa, Purplle, boAt and friends all share this anatomy), so we can point at exact numbers without copying anyone's actual code. Once you can see the pattern here, you'll see it on every real site you open. That recognition is the skill.

Part one — the skeleton
01 / the one box you already have

Your boilerplate is the whole website

Look at what you were given. Nothing about it is incomplete — it is the entire structure every website on earth starts from.

<!-- what you already have --> <!DOCTYPE html> <html lang="en"> <head> <meta charset="UTF-8"> <title>Document</title> </head> <body> <!-- everything you will ever design goes here --> </body> </html>

Every homepage, every product page, every "About Us" you have ever scrolled is just content arranged inside that one pair of <body> tags. A website is not a mysterious canvas — it is boxes, inside boxes, inside the one box you already know how to open.

02 / the only vocabulary you need on day one

Five tags build ninety percent of every website

You don't need forty HTML tags to start. You need these, plus the discipline to use them for what they mean, not just what they look like.

TagWhat lives inside itPlain-English rule
<header>Logo, nav, sometimes a search barThe introduction row. Usually appears once, at the top.
<nav>The list of links to other pagesIf it's a list of places you can go, it's a nav — even if it's just three links.
<main>The actual content of the pageOne per page. Everything that makes this page different from every other page lives here.
<section>A distinct chunk of content inside mainIf you'd give it its own heading, it's a section — hero, categories, testimonials are each their own.
<footer>Sitemap, legal, social, copyrightThe last thing on every page, usually identical across the whole site.

The tag that means "I give up, it's just a box"

When nothing above fits — a card, a row of icons, a wrapper you only need for layout — use <div>. It carries no meaning at all, and that's fine; not everything needs to mean something. A product card is usually an <article> (it makes sense on its own, out of context) with plain <div>s inside it for image/name/price.

Description, ingredients, and reviews are collapsed by default — an accordion. This page has more information than anyone reads in full; collapsing lets a shopper open only what they personally care about.this is a <details><summary> tag in disguise, or a button that toggles a class.

Notice what's missing

No product grid. No "Add to cart." No price anywhere on this page. That absence is deliberate — an About Us page that tries to sell as hard as a product page usually convinces nobody of anything. Different job, different anatomy, on purpose.

09 / this week's exercise

Structure first. Style never comes before structure.

Do not open a design tool yet. This exercise is entirely inside the boilerplate you already have.

StepDoWhy
1Open any real Indian D2C homepage. Screenshot it.A real target, not an imagined one.
2On the screenshot, label every region with a tag name — header, nav, main, section, footer. By hand, on paper.Forces you to see structure, not decoration.
3Open your boilerplate. Build only the skeleton — empty <header>, <nav>, <main> with empty <section>s inside, <footer>. No CSS. No colours.If this doesn't read correctly with zero styling, styling won't save it.
4Fill each section with real text and placeholder boxes for images. Still no CSS.Content decides layout. Never the other way round.
5Only now — add CSS. One property at a time. Screenshot after every single change.You'll always know exactly which line broke something.

What "done" looks like: not a pixel-perfect clone. A page that a stranger could look at and correctly guess which part is the header, which part is the footer, and what the page is for — using nothing but your unstyled HTML, read top to bottom.
