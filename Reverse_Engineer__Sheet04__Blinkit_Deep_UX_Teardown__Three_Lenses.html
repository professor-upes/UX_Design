Field notes · product design · sheet 04

Blinkit, all the way in and all the way out

Sheet 03 ran the three-lens method across ten products at three notes each. This is the opposite move: one product, maximum resolution — every screen from cold open to delivered-and-rated, plus the failure states nobody polishes for a demo. And the timing is unusually good, because in January 2026 the single claim this entire interface was built around was taken away from it. Not by a designer. By the Union Ministry of Labour. What a product does when it can no longer say its own promise out loud is the most instructive thing on this sheet.

Fifteen screens · three audits · every claim graded

How to read this sheet

The three-lens method is assumed known from Sheet 03. Markers on every screen are colour-coded to the lens speaking:

Red — beginner: what is felt before it can be named
Blue — academic: the citable principle underneath
Green — professional: the metric, the ops constraint, the engineering cost

Claims carry an evidence grade wherever it matters: OBSERVED visible in the shipped interface · REPORTED publicly documented, source-backed · INFERRED my professional read of the mechanism, not leaked internals. If a line has no tag, it is design reasoning, not a factual claim.

Every screen is a reconstruction built in HTML/CSS from observation — no screenshots, no scraped assets, no logo or wordmark anywhere. Colours are sampled by eye and are close, not exact. The brand mark inside mockups is rendered as a neutral placeholder. Interface details shift constantly in quick commerce; treat specific ₹ values and screen layouts as a snapshot, not a spec.

Before anything — what changed
00 / the verification pass

The claim this interface was designed to protect no longer exists

Any teardown of this app written before 2026 is now partly wrong, and the specific way it is wrong is the most valuable thing in it.

What the search turned up

  • REPORTED In January 2026, Blinkit removed the "10-minute delivery" claim from its branding across platforms, changing its tagline from "10,000+ products delivered in 10 minutes" to "30,000+ products delivered at your doorstep."
  • REPORTED The change followed discussions between the Union Ministry of Labour and quick-commerce firms about gig-worker safety, after a nationwide delivery-worker strike over the New Year period, which raised road-accident risk and working-hour pressure from time-bound targets.
  • REPORTED Critically: the removal applies to the advertising claim, not the deliveries. Orders still arrive in roughly ten to fifteen minutes. The operation did not change. Only what the company is willing to promise in writing did.
  • REPORTED Blinkit was the first major platform to drop the claim formally; the delivery-time promise remained visible on competitors' store listings at the time of reporting.

This is the cleanest example you will find of a rule from Sheet 03, Uber: sometimes the interface changes because a crisis did. Design did not decide this. Labour organising and a ministry meeting did.

Why this makes Blinkit the right subject for a depth teardown

Most interfaces hide their constraints. This one wears them. The app is the thin visible layer of a physical machine — micro-warehouses, curated stock, dispatch algorithms, riders on two-wheelers — and when any part of that machine moves, you can watch the pixels move with it. The ops-to-interface chain is legible here in a way it simply is not on Notion or Spotify.

And now there is a second layer: a product whose entire positioning was a number, that has been told it may no longer say the number, while continuing to deliver in the number. Every screen after this one should be read with one question running underneath it — how does an interface keep a promise it is not allowed to make?

What breaks if you change one thing

ChangeConsequence
Allow browse-before-location with a national catalogueFriction drops immediately and conversion on first session probably rises. Then a meaningful share of first orders fail at checkout because the items were never in that user's store. You have traded a small entry cost for a large trust cost, at the worst possible moment in the relationship.
Accept coarse (city-level) location instead of a precise pinETA confidence collapses. The header can no longer state a specific number, and the number is the product.
Remove the manual-entry optionNow it is a genuine forced action, and it breaks for anyone whose GPS is unreliable — a real condition in dense Indian urban housing, not an edge case.
Move the "varies by location" line to a terms pageNothing visibly changes, until the first complaint about a price difference between two users in the same colony. Then the line's absence is the whole argument.

What breaks if you change one thing

ChangeConsequence
Use the brand yellow for the ADD controlYellow on white is roughly 1.5:1. The single most-tapped control in the app becomes invisible to anyone in sunlight or with reduced vision. This is why the action colour is green and not the brand colour — a constraint, not a preference.
Show 2 products per row instead of 3Bigger images, easier tapping, and roughly a third fewer items per scroll. In a basket-building app where users add 8–12 items in a few minutes, that is a direct tax on basket size.
Replace the live ETA with "delivered fast"The last remaining specific speed signal disappears, and the product becomes indistinguishable from ordinary grocery delivery. A vague claim is not a differentiator — this is precisely the line the company is now walking after January 2026.
Move "Order again" below the merchandised railsRepeat orders — the cheapest conversions available — now sit behind a scroll. Revenue per session drops without a single visual change anyone would notice.
Drop the persistent cart barCart abandonment stops being visible and starts being silent. In a ten-minute session there is no recovery channel to fall back on.

academic names it The Zeigarnik effect: incomplete tasks hold attention. A three-segment progress bar makes the order into an unfinished object the mind wants closed. This is also why the countdown is phrased as "7 minutes away" rather than "arriving 9:52" — a shrinking number is an unfinished task; a fixed clock time is an appointment. a shrinking number holds you. a timestamp releases you.
3
academic names it Three states — packed, picked up, delivered — is a deliberate compression of a far more complex pipeline. Showing every real stage would be more transparent and much worse: more stages means more visible stalls, and a stalled bar reads as a failure even when the ETA is intact. granularity you can't keep moving is a liability, not honesty
4
professional built it INFERRED The rider is identified by role, not personality — no rating shown, no vehicle number foregrounded, minimal identity. Read this against the reported payout structure and the strike coverage: the interface presents the delivery as a service performed by the platform, not by an individual. That is a positioning choice with real consequences for how the labour behind the speed is perceived. the person carrying the promise is the least designed element on the screen
5
professional built it The add-more-items window is the sharpest commercial idea in the app. INFERRED Marginal cost of extra items on an already-dispatched order is near zero, so incremental AOV here is close to pure margin — and it exists only because the ops model has a real, definable cutoff before the picker finishes. The offer is bounded by a warehouse event, not by a timer someone invented. the best upsell in the app is a warehouse constraint dressed as a courtesy

And then the last screen anyone remembers

Rating and tipping appear after delivery, at the point peak–end theory says will disproportionately shape the memory of the whole order. Two things are worth naming in class:

  • The peak in a fast delivery is usually the doorbell arriving sooner than expected. The end is this screen. A product that nails delivery and then presents a nagging, guilt-shaped tip prompt has spent its own best moment.
  • A tip prompt with pre-selected amounts sits close to two named CCPA patterns at once — basket sneaking if pre-ticked and added by default, confirm shaming if declining requires tapping something like "No, I don't want to support." Neither is necessary. A row of unselected amounts plus a plain "Skip" is the version that survives an audit.

What breaks if you change one thing

Replace the live map with a static "on the way" messageEngineering cost drops sharply. Perceived wait lengthens, because progress stops being observable — the same eleven minutes now feels longer, and the only differentiator is a feeling.

Screen 09 — the states nobody demos
10 / failure, and what it reveals

Design philosophy is most visible where nothing went to plan

Happy paths are designed by everyone. Failure states are designed by teams that have already been burned, and you can usually date the burn.

StateWhat the screen doesThe principleThe mechanism underneath
Item goes out of stock after it is in your cart Item is flagged in the cart with a substitute offered rather than silently dropped, and the total recalculates in view. Visibility of system status. A silent removal is the worst option available — the user discovers it at the door, when nothing can be done. INFERRED Direct consequence of inventory-sync latency. The teaching point worth stopping on

Ask a student to redesign this app and you will get a prettier home screen. Ask them to design the out-of-stock-mid-cart state and you find out whether they understand the business at all. Every row in the table above is a decision that only makes sense once you know what a dark store is — which is why the primer came first.

Audit one — dark patterns
11 / against the Indian taxonomy, not the American one

India has a named list of thirteen. Use it.

Most UX courses teach dark patterns using Western examples and blog-post terminology. India has something better for a classroom: a government-issued list with legal consequences attached, which means students can be marked against a standard rather than an opinion.

The regulatory frame

  • REPORTED The Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023, notified 30 November 2023 under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, identify and prohibit thirteen named dark patterns — including false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing and disguised advertisement.
  • REPORTED In June 2025 the CCPA advised all e-commerce platforms to conduct self-audits within three months and file self-declarations. Around two dozen platforms did so, including quick-commerce operators.
  • REPORTED Independent audits have been considerably less flattering than the self-declarations. A January 2026 LocalCircles audit reported that all major e-commerce and quick-commerce platforms it tested except one failed its dark-pattern test, and separate reporting notes that several companies which self-declared compliance were fined within months.
  • REPORTED Penalties under Section 21 of the Consumer Protection Act run to ₹10 lakh for a first violation and up to ₹50 lakh for each subsequent one — which is, bluntly, small enough that paying can be cheaper than fixing. Teach that tension honestly; it is the reason enforcement remains reactive.

The audit

Verdicts below are my read of the interface as reconstructed and described in this sheet, not a legal finding and not a statement about the company's compliance position. The point of the exercise is the method.

Named patternVerdictReasoning
Drip pricingBorderline — present in structureFees appear only at cart, after basket-building. Mitigated substantially by itemisation, naming, ⓘ explanations and pre-payment disclosure. The structure is drip; the execution is at the honest end of it. This is the single most defensible criticism of the category and the one to spend class time on.
False urgencyLargely absentThe countdowns here are real: the ETA is a computed estimate, and the add-more window closes because a picker finishes. Compare with fabricated "only 2 left!" counters elsewhere. Urgency backed by a warehouse event is not false urgency.
Basket sneakingNot observedNothing is added without a tap. This is the pattern to watch on tip and donation prompts, where the temptation to pre-select is highest and where a fine has already landed elsewhere in Indian e-commerce.
Confirm shamingNot observedDecline paths are neutrally worded. The risk surface is the tip screen; "Skip" is fine, "No, I don't care about my delivery partner" would not be.
Interface interferenceMildManual address entry demoted to underlined text under a full-width primary button. On the right side of the line because it is same-screen, same-viewport, one tap. A useful example precisely because it is a near-miss rather than a violation — students learn the boundary better from these than from egregious cases.
Forced actionStructural, and justifiedLocation is genuinely mandatory. But note the standard: "technically necessary" is a defence only if the necessity is explained to the user. The app does explain it. Remove that one line of copy and the same requirement becomes indefensible.
Subscription trapNot applicable hereAny membership tier should be audited separately, with attention to cancellation-path depth — the classic failure point and a named pattern in its own right.
Disguised advertisementWorth watchingAd monetisation is a stated growth lever for the business. Sponsored placements inside a product grid are indistinguishable from organic ones unless explicitly labelled. This is the pattern most likely to become a live issue as ad revenue grows.
Bait and switchNot observedOut-of-stock is disclosed at tile level and again at cart level rather than substituted silently.
NaggingLow in-appIn-app prompting is restrained. Push notification behaviour is a separate audit surface and outside what can be assessed from screens alone.

The honest summary

This is not a manipulative app. It is a mostly-fair interface with one structural problem — the cost of an order is not knowable until the cart — and that problem is shared across the entire category rather than unique to this product. A student who writes "Blinkit uses dark patterns" has said nothing. A student who writes "the fee stack is disclosed at cart rather than at browse, which is drip pricing in structure though itemised and explained in execution" has written the sentence a regulator, a founder and an examiner would all accept.

Audit two — accessibility
12 / where a yellow brand meets a contrast standard

The palette is not an accident. It is a constraint the team worked around correctly.

Yellow is one of the hardest brand colours to build an accessible interface from, and tracing how this app handles it teaches contrast better than any abstract explanation of the formula.

CheckResultDetail
Header text on brand yellowPassNear-black on yellow, roughly 13:1. Comfortably clears AAA. The correct use of this hue.
Brand yellow as textCorrectly avoidedAround 1.5:1 on white. The app does not do this anywhere I could see — which is a discipline many yellow-branded products fail.
ADD control, green on whitePass AARoughly 4.9:1. Clears the 4.5:1 normal-text threshold; does not clear AAA at 7:1.
ADD control tap targetFailApproximately 62 × 26 px against 44 px (iOS) and 48 dp (Android) minimums. The most-tapped control in the app is roughly half the recommended height. This is the sheet's clearest accessibility finding.
Muted meta textMarginalGrey around #7E808C on white sits near the 4.5:1 line and is used at 8.5–9.5px for pack size and unit info. Small size plus borderline contrast is the most common real-world accessibility failure in dense commerce UIs.
Dynamic typeStructurally constrainedThree-column grids with fixed tile heights have very little room to scale. Large accessibility text sizes will clip or truncate product names well before they break the layout — a density trade-off, not an oversight.
Screen reader experienceNot assessable hereRequires testing on the live app, not from reconstructions. Flag as an open question rather than guessing — the honest answer in an audit is often "we did not test this."
Colour as sole signalMostly avoidedOut-of-stock uses opacity plus an explicit label, not colour alone. Stepper state changes shape as well as fill. Good practice.

The unresolved tension, stated plainly

Enlarging the ADD control to a compliant 48px would cost roughly one product row per viewport. In a basket-building app, products-per-viewport correlates directly with basket size, which correlates with the AOV number the whole business is measured on. So the accessibility fix has a revenue price tag attached, and it is not a small one.

Do not resolve this for students. Make them argue it. The interesting answers are the ones that refuse the binary — expanding the tappable area beyond the visible pill using invisible padding, for instance, which buys compliance without costing a single pixel of layout. That solution exists, and finding it is a better exam answer than picking a side.

Cross-cut — the promise, traced

Count them

Seven of twelve are defending. Three are selling. Two are informing. An interface built to sell groceries turns out, on inspection, to be mostly an interface built to keep one number true.

And then in January 2026 the number was withdrawn from the advertising — while the operation that produces it continued unchanged. So the app now defends a promise it is no longer allowed to make. The in-app estimate persists because an estimate is a different legal object from a claim: one is computed per order and can be missed, the other is advertised to everyone and binds you when it is.

The strategic question to leave a class on: if the number can no longer be advertised, what is the positioning? Reliability rather than raw speed? Assortment breadth — the new tagline leads with 30,000+ products, not with minutes? Watch which one the interface starts foregrounding over the next few quarters. The header is where the answer will appear first, and it will appear there before it appears in any press release.

The exercise
16 / rebuild, don't redesign

The assignment, and how to mark it

Same method as Sheets 02 and 03, with the lens declaration now mandatory and a fourth step that most students will find genuinely hard.

StepDoProduces
1 · CaptureOne screen from any quick-commerce app on your own phone. Not the home screen — pick a screen where a decision happens. The cart is the richest.A constrained target with a real decision in it.
2 · Feel itBefore any analysis, write your raw reaction in one sentence, in your own words. Do this first or it is contaminated.An honest beginner baseline.
3 · Name itFind the citable principle behind that reaction, or a named CCPA pattern. If nothing fits, write "no principle fits — here is what I think is happening instead."Vocabulary that transfers to the next screen.
4 · Follow the metricWhat number is this screen instrumented to move? What did it cost to build? What upstream constraint made this the answer?The step that separates analysis from opinion.
5 · RebuildReconstruct the screen in HTML/CSS from a written table of sampled colours and estimated sizes. Never by tracing.Every gap in understanding, exposed.
6 · Break itChange one token. Screenshot the damage. State which lens notices the break first, and why.Proof the system was understood, not described.

Marking rubric

BandLooks likeThe tell
FailA prettier redesign with no annotationsWords like "cleaner", "more modern", "better UX". No mechanism anywhere. They redesigned the surface without reading the system.
PassAccurate rebuild, beginner and academic notes presentCorrectly names two or three principles. Every note is about the user; none is about the business.
GoodAll three lenses, with at least one real professional noteConnects one visible element to a metric or an ops constraint — and does it without inventing internal numbers.
DistinctionStep 6 done properlyIdentifies a change that looks harmless and traces a non-obvious downstream failure. "Two-column grid improves accessibility and costs a third of basket size" is a distinction answer.
Top of classFinds the trade-off with no clean answer, and refuses to resolve itNames a tension where user interest and business interest genuinely conflict, argues both sides honestly, and proposes a third option that dissolves the binary. The invisible-tap-target fix in the accessibility audit is the model.

The one rule to state before the assignment, not after

Rebuilding an interface for study is legitimate and is how designers have always learned. Shipping a rebuilt interface, or presenting one as your own product design, is not. Draw that line explicitly at the start. Also require that no logos or brand marks appear in submissions — students should reconstruct the system, never the trademark.

Caveats
17 / what this sheet is and is not

Say these out loud before teaching from it

  • Every screen here is a reconstruction built from observation in my own HTML and CSS. No screenshots, no scraped assets, no logo or wordmark. Colours were sampled by eye and are close, not exact. A student who measures a real value and finds it differs has done the exercise correctly.
  • Contrast ratios were computed from those sampled hexes using the standard relative-luminance formula. The direction of every finding is robust; the second decimal place is not. Re-measure against live values before quoting a number in a deliverable.
  • Claims marked REPORTED come from public reporting current at the time of writing. Quick commerce changes fast — fee structures, taglines, category sets and store counts all move quarter to quarter. Re-verify before teaching a specific figure.
  • Claims marked INFERRED are my professional read of the mechanism based on how this category of system is generally built. They are not leaked internals and should never be presented as confirmed company specifics. Where I could not verify something — screen reader behaviour is the clearest example — the honest answer in the audit is "not assessed," and that is what it says.
  • The dark-pattern verdicts are a teaching exercise applied to an interface as reconstructed and described here. They are not a legal finding, not an audit of the live product, and not a statement about any company's compliance position.
  • Where a design choice has both a defensible business reason and a real user cost, this sheet says both and leaves the tension open. That is deliberate. A teardown that resolves every tension has stopped being an analysis and started being an argument.

Where this sits in the series

Sheet 03 was ten products, three notes each — breadth, to install the method. This is one product, fifteen screens and three audits — depth, to show what the method yields when you refuse to stop at the first interesting observation. The method is the transferable thing. Blinkit is just the specimen that happened to be on the table in the year its central claim was taken away from it.
